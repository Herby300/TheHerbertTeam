You found the house.

Your offer was accepted.

The inspection is done.

Then the appraisal comes back...

**Lower than the price you agreed to pay.**

Now what?

First: don't panic.

A low appraisal doesn't automatically mean the deal is dead.

It means we need to look at the numbers, the contract, and the financing strategy before deciding what comes next.

## Why Does the Appraisal Matter?

An appraisal is an independent opinion of the property's value.

When you're financing a home, the lender needs to make sure the property provides enough value to support the mortgage.

[Fannie Mae](https://yourhome.fanniemae.com/understanding-home-appraisals) describes a home appraisal as an independent assessment of the property's value. For conventional purchase financing, the [loan-to-value calculation](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.2-01/loan-value-ltv-ratios) is generally based on the **lower of the purchase price or appraised value**.

That means if you agree to pay $500,000 but the home appraises for $480,000, the lender doesn't simply treat it as a $500,000 property because that's what you offered.

Now we have a **$20,000 appraisal gap** to address.

That doesn't necessarily mean you have to bring another $20,000 to closing.

There may be several options.

The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/my-appraisal-is-less-than-the-sale-price-what-does-that-mean-for-me-en-2007/) explains that a lower appraisal can affect how much the lender is willing to finance and that buyers often use that information to renegotiate.

## Option 1: Ask the Seller to Lower the Price

This is usually the first conversation.

If the contract price is $500,000 and the appraisal comes in at $480,000, the buyer may ask the seller to reduce the price.

Sometimes the seller agrees.

Sometimes they meet somewhere in the middle.

Sometimes they won't reduce it at all.

This is where your real estate agent and lender should be communicating with you about both the contract and the financing.

## Option 2: Buyer and Seller Split the Difference

The solution doesn't always have to be all or nothing.

Using the same example:

Purchase price: **$500,000**

Appraised value: **$480,000**

Gap: **$20,000**

Maybe the seller agrees to reduce the price by $10,000 and the buyer is willing and able to cover the remaining difference.

The exact structure depends on your contract, cash available, loan program, and overall financing.

This is why I like looking at the whole transaction instead of automatically assuming there's only one solution.

## Option 3: Bring Additional Cash

A buyer may choose to pay above the appraised value.

But before doing that, I want to answer two questions:

**Can you afford to?**

And:

**Does it make sense to?**

Let's say you originally planned to put 20% down on a $500,000 home.

That would be a $400,000 loan.

If the appraisal comes in at $480,000 and your goal is still to maintain an 80% loan-to-value ratio, an $80,000 down payment no longer gets you there.

We would need to restructure the numbers.

That could mean bringing additional cash, accepting a different loan-to-value structure if the program allows it, renegotiating the purchase price, or using a combination of strategies.

This is why I don't like the phrase:

**“You just have to cover the appraisal gap.”**

It's not always that simple.

Let's look at the entire financing structure first.

## Option 4: Request a Reconsideration of Value

What happens if we believe the appraisal may be inaccurate?

There is a process called a **Reconsideration of Value**, commonly called an ROV.

This isn't simply:

**“We don't like the number, please raise it.”**

There needs to be a legitimate reason to question the appraisal.

For example:

- Incorrect property information
- Missing or inaccurate features
- Potentially better comparable sales
- Errors in the appraisal
- Other information supporting why the value may not be adequately supported

[Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-1.3-12/appraisal-quality-matters) requires lenders to have a borrower-initiated reconsideration-of-value process when the opinion of value may be unsupported, deficient, or otherwise needs to be reviewed.

The lender can submit the appropriate information through its reconsideration process.

That doesn't guarantee the value will change.

But if there's legitimate evidence that should be reviewed, there is a process for addressing it.

## Option 5: Walk Away — If Your Contract Allows It

Depending on how your purchase contract is written, an appraisal issue may give you options to terminate the transaction.

That's a contract and legal question, so your real estate agent and, when appropriate, attorney should advise you on your rights.

From the mortgage side, my role is to show you what each financing option looks like before you make that decision.

Maybe covering part of the gap makes sense.

Maybe restructuring the loan solves it.

Maybe the seller adjusts the price.

Or maybe walking away is the right decision under the terms of your contract.

The important thing is that you understand your options.

## Should You Ever Pay More Than the Appraised Value?

Sometimes buyers do.

An appraisal is an opinion of market value, not a rule saying you're prohibited from paying more.

There may be situations where a buyer believes a particular property is worth paying a premium for.

Maybe it's the exact neighborhood they want.

Maybe inventory is limited.

Maybe the house has features that are difficult to replace.

That's ultimately the buyer's decision.

But I want buyers to understand exactly what paying above the appraised value does to:

- Cash required at closing
- Loan-to-value
- Mortgage insurance
- Available reserves
- Overall financing strategy

Don't make that decision based only on emotion.

Run the numbers first.

## You Have the Right to See the Appraisal

You should review the appraisal—not just the final value.

For a first-lien mortgage, borrowers generally have the right to receive a free copy of the appraisal or other written valuation obtained by the lender.

The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/do-i-have-the-right-to-receive-a-copy-of-my-home-appraisal-en-192/) confirms that borrowers have the right to a free copy of the home appraisal for a first-lien mortgage.

Look at the details.

Does the square footage look correct?

Bedrooms and bathrooms?

Property condition?

Comparable sales?

Important features?

If something appears wrong, talk to your lender.

## The Best Strategy Starts Before the Appraisal

Here's something I tell buyers before we're ever dealing with a low appraisal:

**Don't use every dollar you have just to get into the house.**

Having reserves gives you flexibility.

If you're already using every available dollar for your down payment and closing costs, even a small appraisal issue can suddenly become a major problem.

That's one reason I don't automatically recommend putting every possible dollar toward the down payment.

Your mortgage strategy should leave room for real life—and sometimes real estate transactions throw us a curveball.

## A Low Appraisal Doesn't Automatically Kill the Deal

If the appraisal comes in low, there are usually several conversations to have before assuming the transaction is over.

We can look at:

1. Renegotiating the purchase price
2. Splitting the appraisal gap
3. Adjusting the financing
4. Bringing additional cash
5. Requesting a Reconsideration of Value when appropriate
6. Reviewing your contract options

The right answer depends on the specific property, appraisal, loan program, contract, and your financial goals.

**Don't just react to the appraisal number. Build a strategy around it.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What happens if a home appraises for less than the purchase price?

A lower appraisal can affect the financing because the lender may use the lower appraised value when determining the loan-to-value ratio. Buyers may be able to renegotiate the price, adjust the financing, bring additional cash, request a reconsideration of value, or exercise applicable contract rights.

### Does the buyer automatically have to pay the entire appraisal gap?

No. The solution depends on negotiations, the purchase contract, loan program, available cash, and financing structure.

### Can an appraisal be challenged?

Yes. If there are legitimate concerns about the accuracy or support for the appraisal, borrowers can ask their lender about requesting a Reconsideration of Value.

### Can the seller lower the price after a low appraisal?

Yes, if the buyer and seller agree to amend the purchase contract.

### Can I still buy a house for more than its appraised value?

Potentially, but paying above the appraised value can affect the cash required and financing structure. Buyers should review the numbers with their lender and the contract implications with their real estate professional before deciding.
