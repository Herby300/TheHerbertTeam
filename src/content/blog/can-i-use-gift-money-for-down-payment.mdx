For many homebuyers, the biggest obstacle is not the monthly mortgage payment.

It is the upfront cash needed for the down payment, closing costs, prepaid taxes and insurance, and financial reserves.

That leads to an important question:

**“Can my family help me with the money I need to buy a home?”**

Potentially, yes.

Many mortgage programs allow eligible gift funds. But the money cannot simply appear in your account without explanation.

The lender may need to verify:

- Who provided the gift
- The donor’s relationship to you
- That repayment is not expected
- Where the money came from
- How the money was transferred
- Whether the selected loan program permits the gift
- Which purchase expenses the gift may cover

> **Quick answer:** Gift funds may be used for a down payment, closing costs, or reserves under certain mortgage programs. The donor, purpose, source, transfer, property, and required borrower contribution must meet the rules of the selected loan. The Fannie Mae rules described below apply to conventional loans sold to Fannie Mae; Freddie Mac, FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, and portfolio loans have their own requirements.

The best time to discuss gift funds is during preapproval—not a few days before closing.

## What Are Mortgage Gift Funds?

Mortgage gift funds are money provided to a homebuyer without an expectation of repayment.

That last part matters.

If the buyer is expected to repay the donor, the money is not truly a gift. It may instead be borrowed funds and could create an additional debt that must be disclosed and evaluated. The [CFPB notes](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/where-can-i-get-money-for-a-down-payment-on-a-home-en-123/) that you may need to prove where the money came from and sign a statement that it is a gift, not a loan, and that borrowing your down payment increases your overall debt and monthly payments.

A legitimate gift may help cover:

- Down payment
- Closing costs
- Prepaid property taxes
- Prepaid homeowners insurance
- Initial escrow deposits
- Discount points, when otherwise permitted
- Financial reserves, when permitted by the program

The allowable uses depend on the loan program and transaction.

The lender must review the actual purchase, property, donor, documentation, and mortgage requirements.

## Who Can Give You Gift Funds?

The answer depends on the mortgage program.

Under current [Fannie Mae conventional guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-04/personal-gifts), acceptable donors can include qualifying relatives and certain non-relatives who share a defined familial, domestic-partner, former-relative, fiancé, or long-standing familial-like or mentorship relationship with the borrower.

An acceptable donor cannot be—or be affiliated with—the builder, developer, real estate agent, or another interested party to the transaction. One exception: a property seller who is otherwise an acceptable donor (a parent selling to a child, for example) and is not affiliated with another interested party may provide a gift.

Other mortgage programs define acceptable donors differently.

Do not assume that money from a friend, employer, seller, real estate professional, business associate, or unrelated third party will qualify as a personal gift.

Identify the donor during preapproval so the lender can confirm eligibility before anyone transfers money.

## Can a Gift Cover the Entire Down Payment?

Sometimes.

Under [current Fannie Mae guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-04/personal-gifts), all of the funds needed to close can come from an eligible gift when:

- The loan-to-value ratio is 80% or less on a one- to four-unit principal residence or a second home, or
- The property is a one-unit principal residence, at any loan-to-value ratio.

Above 80% loan-to-value on a two- to four-unit principal residence or a second home, Fannie Mae requires the borrower to contribute 5% from their own funds. Gift funds can then supplement the rest of the down payment, closing costs, and reserves.

Fannie Mae also has a special rule for a donor who has lived with the borrower for the past 12 months and will continue to live with them in the new principal residence: that donor’s gift can be treated as the borrower’s own funds, with documentation of the shared residence.

Personal gifts are not allowed on an investment-property transaction under Fannie Mae guidance.

Different rules can also apply to affordable-lending programs such as HomeReady, FHA, VA, or USDA financing, and jumbo or portfolio loans.

The practical answer is not:

**“All gifts always work.”**

It is:

**“Let’s match the gift to the borrower, property, occupancy, and loan program.”**

## What Is a Gift Letter?

A gift letter documents that the money is being given to the borrower rather than loaned.

Under current Fannie Mae guidance, the signed gift letter must identify:

- The donor
- The donor’s address
- The donor’s telephone number
- The donor’s relationship to the borrower
- The actual or maximum gift amount
- A statement that no repayment is expected

Gifts from a trust or estate have an additional requirement: the letter must be signed by the donor and identify the trust or estate account.

The lender or loan program may require additional information or a particular form.

A gift letter does not replace the transfer documentation.

The lender may still need to verify that the donor had sufficient funds and that the money moved through an acceptable, traceable path.

## How Should the Gift Money Be Transferred?

The safest answer is:

**Follow the lender’s instructions before moving the money.**

Under Fannie Mae’s current guidance, acceptable documentation may include:

- The donor’s check and the borrower’s deposit record
- The donor’s withdrawal record and the borrower’s deposit record
- Evidence of an electronic transfer from the donor to the borrower
- Evidence of an electronic transfer from the donor directly to the closing agent
- The donor’s check to the closing agent
- A settlement statement showing receipt of the gift

When the gift is not transferred until closing, Fannie Mae requires the lender to document that the donor gave the closing agent the funds by electronic transfer, certified check, cashier’s check, or another official check.

A direct, well-documented transfer is generally easier to review than a trail involving cash withdrawals, multiple accounts, payment apps, or transfers through other people.

Before moving the funds, ask:

1. Should the money go to my account or directly to the closing agent?
2. What records must the donor retain?
3. What records must I provide?
4. Does the transfer need to occur before underwriting or only before closing?
5. Will the lender need to verify the donor’s account?
6. How should the donor label or reference the transfer?

A five-minute conversation before the transfer can prevent days of document cleanup.

## Why Cash Gifts Can Create Problems

Suppose your parents give you $20,000 in cash.

You deposit the money into your checking account and then tell the lender it was a gift.

The intent may be completely legitimate.

But cash is difficult to trace back to the donor’s account and establish through normal financial records.

The lender may not be able to verify:

- That the donor had the funds
- That the funds came from the stated donor
- That the money was not borrowed
- That an interested party did not provide it
- That the source was otherwise eligible

Do not accept or deposit a large cash gift for a home purchase without first speaking with the lender.

A traceable bank transfer, official check, or permitted direct transfer to the closing agent normally creates a clearer paper trail.

## Does Gift Money Have to Sit in My Account for 60 Days?

Not necessarily.

There is no universal rule that every gift must sit untouched in the borrower’s account for exactly 60 days.

What matters is whether the lender can verify the funds under the selected program and documentation requirements.

Recent gift deposits may require:

- A signed gift letter
- Evidence of the donor’s funds
- Evidence of the transfer
- An updated borrower bank statement or transaction history
- Confirmation that no repayment is expected

Waiting does not convert borrowed, cash, or otherwise ineligible funds into an acceptable gift.

Do not move money early simply to make the transaction less visible. Disclose the source and document it correctly.

## Can Gift Funds Be Used for Earnest Money?

Potentially.

If a donor provides money that is used for the earnest-money deposit, the lender may need to document the gift and the transfer to the escrow holder, builder, real estate brokerage, title company, or other approved recipient.

Under [Fannie Mae’s earnest-money guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-09/earnest-money-deposit), when the deposit counts toward the borrower’s required contribution, the lender must verify that it came from an acceptable source, and receipt of the deposit is typically verified with a canceled check or a written statement from the deposit holder.

That means the paper trail should begin when the earnest money is paid—not weeks later when the lender starts reviewing the file.

Keep:

- The gift letter
- The donor’s transfer record
- The earnest-money receipt
- The borrower’s account record, if the money passed through the borrower
- The executed purchase contract
- Any escrow or title confirmation

Tell the lender when the earnest money came from a gift.

## What Is a Gift of Equity?

A gift of equity is different from a cash gift.

It generally occurs when an acceptable donor sells a property to the borrower and gives the borrower a portion of the seller’s equity as a credit in the transaction.

For example:

A parent owns a home worth $400,000 and agrees to sell it to an adult child. Part of the parent’s equity may be structured as a gift of equity, subject to the mortgage, appraisal, contract, title, and program requirements.

Current [Fannie Mae guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-05/gifts-equity) permits an eligible gift of equity on principal-residence and second-home purchases. It may be used toward the down payment and closing costs, including prepaid items, but it cannot be used as financial reserves. The acceptable-donor and borrower-contribution rules for personal gifts also apply.

The loan file generally needs a signed gift letter and settlement documentation showing the gift of equity.

A gift of equity should be planned with the lender, real estate professionals, title company, and appropriate tax or legal advisors before the contract is finalized.

## Can the Donor Borrow the Gift Money?

The lender may need to determine whether the donor had an acceptable source for the funds and whether the borrower has any repayment obligation.

A gift is not acceptable if the homebuyer is secretly expected to repay it.

Do not create an informal arrangement in which:

- The donor uses a personal loan but expects you to make the payments
- You sign an undisclosed promissory note
- You agree to reimburse the donor after closing
- The money actually comes from an interested party
- Another person temporarily routes money through the donor’s account

If repayment is expected, tell the lender.

Some borrowed funds may be eligible under separate rules. For example, Fannie Mae permits [borrowed funds secured by an asset](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-15/borrowed-funds-secured-asset) the borrower owns. But borrowed money should never be disguised as a gift.

## A Practical Gift-Fund Example

Assume Maya is buying her first home for $350,000.

Her loan requires a $12,250 down payment, and she expects approximately $8,500 in closing costs and prepaid expenses.

Maya has $10,000 saved.

Her parents would like to contribute $15,000.

Instead of transferring the money immediately, Maya first sends the lender:

- The proposed donor names
- Their relationship to her
- The expected gift amount
- The account from which the gift will be sent
- The approximate transfer date

The lender confirms that her parents are acceptable donors under the selected program and provides the gift-letter requirements.

The parents sign the letter and send the funds through the instructed transfer method. Maya and her parents keep the transfer records.

The lender can now evaluate:

1. Whether the gift is eligible
2. Whether Maya has enough total funds to close
3. Whether any reserves are required
4. Whether Maya must contribute her own funds
5. Whether the final gift amount matches the underwriting file
6. Whether the transfer is fully documented

The key was not simply that Maya’s parents had money.

The key was planning and documenting the gift before it moved.

## Common Gift-Fund Mistakes

### Moving the money before speaking with the lender

This can create avoidable documentation problems, especially when the funds pass through multiple accounts.

### Calling a loan a gift

A side agreement requiring repayment changes the analysis and must be disclosed.

### Using cash

Cash often lacks the documentation needed to verify the source and transfer.

### Assuming anyone can be the donor

Acceptable-donor definitions vary by mortgage program.

### Changing the amount at the last minute

If the actual gift differs from the amount that was underwritten, the lender may need to rerun the automated underwriting or update documents.

### Sending the money through a payment app

Some transfers may not show enough information to verify the sender, recipient, account, and source.

### Forgetting that earnest money was gifted

The lender may still need to document the original source and transfer.

### Treating a gift of equity like a cash gift

A gift of equity involves the property seller’s equity and different closing documentation.

## Gift-Fund Checklist

Before the gift is transferred, confirm:

- The loan program
- Property type
- Occupancy
- Eligible donor
- Donor relationship
- Gift amount
- Permitted use
- Required borrower contribution
- Reserve requirements
- Gift-letter format
- Evidence needed from the donor
- Transfer method
- Transfer timing
- Closing-agent instructions
- Earnest-money treatment
- Whether the donor is also the seller
- Whether tax or legal advice is needed

Keep every page of the relevant statements and every transfer confirmation until the mortgage is complete.

Do not alter, recreate, or hide financial documents.

## Does the Donor Owe Gift Tax?

Mortgage eligibility and tax treatment are separate questions.

The lender’s approval of gift funds does not determine whether the donor must file a gift-tax return or whether any tax is due.

Gift-tax rules can depend on the amount, donor, recipient, timing, prior gifts, and available exclusions.

The buyer and donor should discuss tax questions with a qualified tax professional.

The mortgage team’s role is to determine whether the funds satisfy the loan’s asset and documentation requirements.

## The Bottom Line

Gift funds can make homeownership possible sooner.

But the money should be treated as part of the mortgage plan—not as a last-minute transfer between family members.

Before anyone moves the funds, determine:

- Whether the donor is eligible
- Whether the gift can cover the intended expense
- Whether you must contribute your own money
- What the gift letter must say
- How the transfer should occur
- Which records the donor and borrower must keep

The cleanest gift is disclosed early, transferred directly, and documented completely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can my parents give me money for a house down payment?

Potentially. Parents are commonly eligible donors, but the gift must satisfy the selected mortgage program’s documentation, source, transfer, property, occupancy, and borrower-contribution requirements.

### Does gift money have to be repaid?

No. A true gift must not require repayment. If repayment is expected, disclose the arrangement to the lender so it can be evaluated correctly.

### Can gift funds cover all of my down payment?

Sometimes. Current Fannie Mae guidance permits all funds needed to come from an eligible gift on a one-unit principal residence, or on any eligible property at 80% loan-to-value or less. Two- to four-unit principal residences and second homes above 80% loan-to-value require a 5% borrower contribution, and other programs may differ.

### What must a mortgage gift letter include?

The requirements vary, but current Fannie Mae guidance requires the donor’s signature, the actual or maximum gift amount, a statement that repayment is not expected, the donor’s name, address, and telephone number, and the donor’s relationship to the borrower.

### Does the donor have to provide a bank statement?

The lender must verify the donor’s availability of funds or the completed transfer. The exact documentation may include account records, transfer evidence, checks, deposit records, or closing documentation.

### Can the donor wire the money directly to the title or escrow company?

Potentially. Direct transfers to the closing agent may be permitted when they are completed and documented according to the lender’s and closing agent’s instructions.

### Can I use cash given to me by a family member?

Cash can be difficult or impossible to document adequately. Speak with the lender before accepting or depositing cash intended for the purchase.

### Can gift funds be used for closing costs?

Potentially. Eligible gift funds may be used for closing costs under many mortgage programs, subject to the program and transaction requirements.

### Can gift funds be used for reserves?

Sometimes. Fannie Mae permits eligible personal gifts to fund reserves in qualifying transactions, but a gift of equity cannot be used for reserves. Other programs may differ.

### What is a gift of equity?

A gift of equity occurs when an eligible property seller gives the buyer a portion of the seller’s equity as a credit in the purchase. It requires specific contract and closing documentation.

### Should the gift be transferred before I apply?

Not without instructions. Identify the proposed donor and gift during preapproval, then follow the lender’s directions for the letter, source documentation, timing, and transfer.
