Buying a home while your divorce is still pending may be possible.

But the mortgage qualification can be more complicated than a normal purchase because the lender has to look at your financial situation **as it exists today**, not simply how everyone expects it to look after the divorce is final.

That means questions like:

- Who is responsible for the marital-home mortgage right now?
- Which joint debts are still legally yours?
- Are you paying or receiving support?
- Which assets are actually available for the new purchase?
- Has a separation agreement or temporary order been entered?
- Will your spouse need to sign any title or lien documents?
- Can you afford both the current obligations and the proposed new home?

The answer is not automatically “wait until the divorce is over.”

But it also should not be:

**“I’ll just buy now and sort the divorce out later.”**

The best approach is to review the mortgage plan before you make an offer.

## Can You Get a Mortgage While You Are Still Married?

Potentially, yes.

Being married or separated does not automatically prevent you from applying for a mortgage in your own name. Under federal fair-lending rules, a lender can't deny you individual credit because of your marital status, and if you qualify on your own, your spouse generally can't be required to co-sign the loan ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/if-i-am-married-can-a-lender-or-broker-turn-down-my-application-for-a-mortgage-or-home-equity-loan-en-351/)).

The lender still evaluates the normal qualification factors:

- Income
- Employment
- Credit
- Monthly debts
- Assets
- Down payment
- Property
- Loan program

But a pending divorce can add another layer because some financial obligations may not be settled yet.

For example, you may still be jointly responsible for the mortgage on the marital home even if your spouse is living there and making the payment.

Until the mortgage or legal responsibility is resolved, the lender may need to account for that obligation when qualifying you for the new home.

## The Marital-Home Mortgage May Still Count

This is usually one of the first things I review.

Suppose you and your spouse jointly own a home with a $3,000 monthly housing payment.

You move out and your spouse stays in the house.

Your spouse has been making the entire payment.

Can we automatically ignore that $3,000 when qualifying you for another mortgage?

Not necessarily.

If you are still legally obligated on the mortgage, the lender must determine how that debt should be treated under the selected loan program.

Current Fannie Mae guidance contains specific exceptions. For example:

- **Property-settlement buyout.** If your spouse buys out your interest, as often happens in a divorce, but the lender doesn't release you from the mortgage, the payment doesn't have to be counted once the lender has documentation confirming the title transfer ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-06](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-06/qualifying-impact-other-real-estate-owned)).
- **Pending sale.** If the home is under contract but won't close before your new purchase, the current payment can be excluded with the executed sales contract and confirmation that financing contingencies have cleared ([B3-6-06](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-06/qualifying-impact-other-real-estate-owned)).
- **Payment made by a co-borrower.** If your spouse is also obligated on the mortgage and has been making the payments, the payment may be excluded with 12 months of canceled checks or bank statements from your spouse showing no late payments ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-05](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations)).

But those exceptions depend on documentation and the actual transaction structure. They are Fannie Mae conventional-loan rules; FHA, VA, USDA, and other programs have their own requirements, and an individual lender can apply stricter standards.

During a pending divorce, the property may not yet have been transferred and the settlement may not yet be final.

That is why we need to review the current mortgage, title, separation documents, and proposed divorce plan before assuming the payment can be excluded.

## Joint Credit Cards and Other Debts Can Matter Too

The same issue can apply to other joint obligations.

Examples include:

- Auto loans
- Credit cards
- Personal loans
- Home-equity loans
- Student loans
- Lines of credit

The divorce may eventually assign a debt to one spouse.

But before that happens, the lender may still see you as legally responsible for the obligation.

A private agreement between spouses saying:

**“You pay that card and I’ll pay this one”**

does not change the creditor relationship. Even a divorce decree that assigns a debt to one spouse doesn't automatically release the other; creditors can generally still collect from anyone whose name is on the account until the creditor releases them or the debt is refinanced ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-contact-me-about-a-divorce-en-1413/)).

On the mortgage side, a court order can still help. Under Fannie Mae guidelines, when a debt has been assigned to your spouse by court order, the lender isn't required to count that payment, although your payment history on the debt before the assignment still matters ([B3-6-05](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations)). An informal agreement between spouses doesn't carry the same weight.

The mortgage qualification has to be based on the documentation available at the time.

## What About Child Support or Spousal Support?

Support can affect both sides of the mortgage equation.

If a divorce decree, separation agreement, or other written legal agreement requires you to pay support, that obligation may need to be included in the mortgage analysis. Under Fannie Mae guidelines, required alimony, child support, or separate maintenance payments that will continue for more than ten months must be counted; voluntary payments don't have to be ([B3-6-05](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations)).

If you will receive support and want to use it as qualifying income, the lender will need to verify the amount, payment history, and expected continuance. Fannie Mae, for example, generally requires a copy of the decree or separation agreement, six months of full, regular, and timely payments, and evidence that the income will continue for at least three years. If you're separated without an agreement that specifies support, proposed or voluntary payments shouldn't be counted as income ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-3.4-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.4-02/alimony-child-support-equalization-payments-or-separate-maintenance)). Other loan programs have their own rules.

This is one reason buying before the divorce is final can be tricky.

The support arrangement may still be temporary.

Payments may not have begun.

Or the final amount may still be changing.

Rather than assuming what the final decree will say, I want to underwrite the actual documentation we have today.

## Where Is the Down Payment Coming From?

This is another important question.

During divorce, money that appears available in a bank account may still be part of the marital estate.

That becomes a legal issue, not just a mortgage issue.

From the lending side, I need to know:

- Which accounts will fund the purchase?
- Are the funds accessible?
- Can they be documented?
- Will the divorce agreement affect them?
- Does anyone else have an ownership interest in the funds?
- Will the title or escrow company require additional documentation?

The divorce attorney should advise the client about whether assets may legally be used.

The lender determines whether the funds meet mortgage requirements.

Those are separate questions.

## Community-Property and Title Issues Can Matter

A person may qualify for a mortgage individually while still married.

But depending on state law and the property involved, the lender or title company may still require information or signatures from the spouse for certain title, lien, or collateral purposes. Federal rules allow a lender to require a spouse's signature on the documents needed under state law to create a valid lien or pass clear title ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-spouse-have-to-co-sign-my-mortgage-loan-en-359/)).

This is especially important in community-property situations. A lender may ask about your spouse if you live in a community-property state, or if you rely on property located in one to repay the loan ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/if-i-am-married-can-a-lender-or-broker-turn-down-my-application-for-a-mortgage-or-home-equity-loan-en-351/)).

That does **not** necessarily mean the spouse becomes a borrower on the new mortgage.

It means marital status and property rights can affect how the lender secures its interest in the property.

This is an area where the mortgage professional, title company, and divorce attorney should coordinate rather than make assumptions.

## A Simple Example

Assume Sarah is separated and wants to buy a $450,000 home.

She earns enough income to qualify for the new mortgage.

But she is still a borrower on the marital-home mortgage with a $3,200 monthly payment.

The divorce is expected to award that home to her spouse, who will eventually refinance.

That may happen.

But if it has not happened yet, the mortgage lender cannot simply pretend Sarah is already off the loan.

We first need to determine:

1. How the current $3,200 mortgage must be treated
2. Whether there is documentation allowing an underwriting exception
3. Whether Sarah can qualify while carrying both housing obligations
4. Whether temporary or permanent support must be included
5. Whether her down-payment funds are available for the transaction
6. Whether any spouse-related title requirements apply

Maybe the purchase works today.

Maybe the marital home needs to be sold or refinanced first.

Maybe the divorce needs to reach a particular milestone.

The point is to determine that **before Sarah is under contract on another house.**

## Should You Get Preapproved Before the Divorce Is Final?

I think this is often a very good idea.

But I view it as a **Mortgage Feasibility Review**, not simply issuing a maximum purchase-price letter.

We can model:

### Scenario 1: Buying while the divorce is pending

What debts and obligations have to be counted today?

### Scenario 2: Buying after the divorce is final

How does qualification change once the property division and support obligations are documented?

### Scenario 3: Buying after the marital home is sold or refinanced

What happens once the existing mortgage obligation has been resolved?

Comparing those scenarios can help you decide whether buying now makes sense or whether waiting creates a significantly better mortgage position.

## A Note for Divorce Attorneys and Mediators

When a client wants to purchase another home before the divorce is final, useful mortgage questions include:

- Will the client remain obligated on the marital-home mortgage?
- Is the home expected to be sold, refinanced, or transferred?
- Is there a realistic timeline for that to happen?
- Which joint debts remain outstanding?
- Are temporary support payments already required?
- Will the client need support income to qualify?
- Which assets will fund the new purchase?
- Could the proposed purchase affect the marital-property discussion?
- Are there state-specific title or spousal-signature requirements?
- Can the client actually qualify today?

The attorney determines whether purchasing another property is legally appropriate.

My role is different.

I help answer:

**“If the client is allowed to buy, does the mortgage actually work?”**

That distinction is important.

## Do Not Base a Purchase on What You Think the Final Divorce Will Say

One of the easiest mistakes to make is qualifying yourself based on the future.

**“My spouse is getting the house.”**

**“That debt won’t be mine anymore.”**

**“I’ll be receiving support.”**

**“I’ll have my share of the equity.”**

All of those things may eventually happen.

But the lender generally has to underwrite the documented financial situation that exists when you apply and close.

If the divorce changes that situation before closing, we may need to update the mortgage analysis.

That is why communication between the borrower, lender, attorney, and title professional matters.

## The Bottom Line

You may be able to buy another home before your divorce is final.

The question is not simply:

**“Am I allowed to apply for a mortgage?”**

The better question is:

**“What does my financial picture look like today, and what will the lender have to count?”**

Before making an offer, review:

- The marital-home mortgage
- Joint debts
- Support obligations
- Available assets
- Credit
- Income
- Proposed new payment
- Divorce documents
- Title requirements
- The expected timeline for resolving the existing home

A Mortgage Feasibility Review can compare buying now versus waiting until specific divorce or housing milestones are complete.

Sometimes buying now works perfectly well.

Sometimes waiting creates a much cleaner and stronger mortgage position.

The goal is to know which situation you are in **before you commit to another home.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I buy a house before my divorce is final?

Potentially. A pending divorce does not automatically prevent mortgage qualification, but the lender must evaluate your current income, debts, existing mortgage obligations, assets, support requirements, credit, and the applicable loan-program rules.

### Does my current marital-home mortgage count against me?

It may. If you remain legally obligated on the mortgage, the payment may need to be included unless the selected mortgage program provides an applicable exception and the required documentation is available.

### Can I buy a house in only my name while I am still married?

Potentially. A borrower may qualify individually, although state law, community-property rules, title requirements, and the lender’s ability to secure the property can affect what information or spouse signatures are required.

### Can I use support income before the divorce is final?

Potentially, if there is qualifying legal documentation and the income meets the applicable mortgage program’s requirements for receipt history and continuance. A future expected support award should not automatically be assumed as qualifying income.

### What happens to joint credit-card or auto-loan debt?

If you remain legally responsible for the debt, it may affect mortgage qualification even if you and your spouse have informally agreed that the other person will make the payment.

### Should I wait until the divorce is final before buying?

Not necessarily. The better approach is to compare qualification today with qualification after the divorce, sale, refinance, or other expected milestone. A Mortgage Feasibility Review can help identify which timing makes the most sense.

### Should my divorce attorney be involved before I buy?

Yes. Your attorney should advise you about the legal and property consequences of purchasing real estate while the divorce is pending. Your mortgage professional should evaluate whether the financing works.
