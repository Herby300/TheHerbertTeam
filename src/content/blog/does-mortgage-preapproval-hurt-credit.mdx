This is one of the first questions I hear from people who are ready to look at houses but not ready to “mess up” their credit.

**Does getting preapproved for a mortgage hurt your credit?**

Usually, much less than people think.

A real preapproval typically involves a credit inquiry. That inquiry can cause a small, temporary dip.

What I don’t want is for that small dip to keep someone from doing the one step that actually protects them: finding out what they can comfortably buy **before** they fall in love with a house.

The credit question matters.

The bigger question is:

**What is the cost of shopping for a home without a real preapproval?**

---

## The Short Answer

A mortgage preapproval can affect your credit score.

For most buyers, the impact is:

- Small
- Temporary
- Far less important than having a verified approval before you write an offer

If a lender only runs a soft credit check, that usually does not change your score at all.

If a lender runs a hard inquiry as part of a real preapproval, the typical drop is often only a few points. It is not the same as opening a new credit card, financing furniture, or taking out a car loan.

Those are the moves that can actually create problems.

---

## Prequalification and Preapproval Are Not the Same Thing

This distinction matters, because the credit impact is not identical.

A **prequalification** is usually an estimate. You tell a lender your income, debts, and down payment. They run the numbers and give you a ballpark.

Sometimes there is no credit pull.

Sometimes there is a soft pull.

A **preapproval** is different.

A real preapproval means the lender reviewed your documents, pulled credit, and checked whether you actually qualify. Your pay stubs, W-2s, bank statements, and credit report were part of the file. That is the letter a listing agent takes seriously.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s guide to getting a preapproval letter](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/explore/get-a-preapproval-letter/) is a useful official overview of what that letter is — and what it is not. A preapproval is not a guaranteed loan offer, but it tells a seller you are likely able to get financing.

I wrote more about this in [Seven Mistakes First-Time Buyers Make](/blog/first-time-buyer-mistakes). The short version is:

**Prequalification is a conversation. Preapproval is a file review.**

If you are asking whether “getting preapproved” will hurt your credit, you are usually asking about the real version.

That’s the one I want buyers to get.

## Soft Pull vs. Hard Inquiry

Credit checks come in two basic flavors.

The [CFPB explains the difference](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-inquiry-en-1317/) this way:

A **hard inquiry** is typically what happens after you apply for credit. Lenders use it to decide whether to approve the loan. Hard inquiries can affect your credit score.

A **soft inquiry** can happen when you check your own report, when an existing creditor reviews your file, or when a company prescreens you. Soft inquiries do not affect your credit scores.

Some lenders start with a soft pull. That can be useful for a first look.

A seller-ready preapproval usually goes further. The lender needs an actual credit report to underwrite the file, price the loan, and write a letter that means something.

So yes:

**Expect a hard inquiry if you want a real preapproval.**

That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to understand what that inquiry does, and what it does not do.

## How Much Does a Mortgage Inquiry Actually Drop Your Score?

There is no universal number.

The effect depends on:

- Your current score
- How much credit you already have
- How many recent inquiries are already on the report
- The scoring model the lender is using

In practice, a single mortgage inquiry often moves a score by only a few points. For many borrowers, it is closer to a small nudge than a real setback.

A few points is usually not what stops someone from qualifying.

What can stop someone is:

- A collection they did not know about
- Credit-card utilization that is higher than they thought
- A recent late payment
- A new auto loan they took out “just before shopping”
- A credit report error that would have been easy to fix with time

That is one of the hidden benefits of getting preapproved early.

The pull may shave a few points.

It can also surface a problem while you still have time to deal with it.

## Mortgage Shopping Is Not Supposed to Stack Inquiry After Inquiry

This is the part most people do not hear clearly enough.

Credit-scoring models expect you to shop mortgage rates.

If you talk to several lenders in a short window, those mortgage inquiries are generally treated as **one shopping event**, not five separate applications.

The [CFPB says](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-exactly-happens-when-a-mortgage-lender-checks-my-credit-en-2005/) that within a **45-day window**, multiple credit checks from mortgage lenders are recorded as a single inquiry. Some scoring models use a shorter window, often in the 14-to-45-day range.

That is why I tell buyers:

**Do your lender shopping in a defined window. Don’t stretch it out over months.**

Comparing two or three real options is not reckless.

It is how you avoid accepting the first quote you hear.

What I don’t want is:

- A preapproval in January
- Another one in April
- Another one in July
- Then a new car loan in August
- Then an actual offer in September

That pattern can create a mess.

A focused shopping period usually does not.

## What Actually Hurts Your Credit During a Home Purchase

The preapproval inquiry is rarely the problem.

These are the things that create real damage:

**New installment debt.** A car loan, furniture financing, or a “90 days same as cash” package can change your debt-to-income ratio. I have seen that break a loan close to closing.

**Maxed-out credit cards.** Utilization matters. If you put moving costs, deposits, and new furniture on cards before closing, the score can drop more than the original mortgage inquiry ever did.

**Late payments.** One 30-day late can hurt far more than a hard inquiry.

**Closing old accounts.** People sometimes “clean up” their credit by closing cards. That can reduce available credit and shorten average account age.

**Co-signing.** Becoming responsible for someone else’s debt can change both your score and your qualifying ratios.

If you want to protect your credit while buying a house, the rule is simpler than most people expect:

**Get preapproved. Then change as little as possible until you have keys.**

## Your Credit Gets Checked More Than Once

This surprises people.

The first pull is usually at preapproval.

Then, before closing, the lender typically pulls credit again.

That second look is one reason the “don’t change anything” rule matters so much.

A small inquiry at the beginning of the process is expected.

A new truck payment three weeks before closing is not.

If your score dips a few points at preapproval and then stays stable, that is a normal path.

If the file looks different at closing than it did at preapproval, we now have a problem to solve under a deadline.

## How Long Does a Preapproval Last?

The CFPB notes that a preapproval letter can have an expiration date, typically **30 to 60 days**. In practice, I see most files stay useful in that range, and sometimes a little longer, depending on the lender, the loan program, and how stale the documents become.

After that, income documents, bank statements, and credit may need to be updated.

That does not mean you should wait until the week you want to write an offer.

It means:

**Get preapproved when you are ready to shop, and keep the file current while you are shopping.**

If you are three to six months away, a credit review can still be valuable. We can look at score, utilization, collections, and pricing tiers before you are under contract.

If you are actively touring homes, I want a current preapproval in hand.

## Does Checking With Multiple Lenders Hurt You?

Done the right way, no.

Mortgage shopping is supposed to happen.

I would rather a buyer compare:

- Monthly payment
- Cash to close
- Discount points
- Lender credits
- Mortgage insurance
- How the file will actually be underwritten

than lock in the first number they see.

The key is the window.

Shop lenders close together.

Don’t open unrelated credit while you are shopping.

And make sure you are comparing the same loan type, down payment, and property scenario. A lower rate on a completely different program is not a better quote. It is a different loan.

## What If My Credit Is Not Perfect Yet?

Then getting preapproved can be even more useful.

A preapproval is not only a permission slip to shop.

It is a diagnostic.

I can often tell pretty quickly whether the issue is:

- Utilization that can improve in 30 to 60 days
- A collection that needs a plan
- A reporting error worth disputing
- A thin file
- A recent late payment that needs time
- A program mismatch, where FHA, VA, or another option fits better than the loan someone assumed they needed

Waiting to “get credit perfect” before talking to a lender can cost more than the inquiry.

If you are close to a pricing tier, I would rather know that now.

A few extra points can change the rate.

A few extra months of guessing can change the house you buy.

## The Cost of Not Getting Preapproved

I watch this play out every year.

Someone avoids the credit pull.

They tour houses anyway.

They fall in love with a payment they built in a national calculator that did not include Texas taxes and insurance.

Then they write an offer with a weak letter, or no letter.

Or they get far enough into the process to learn that a collection, a debt ratio, or a credit-score tier is going to be a problem.

Now they are negotiating under pressure.

A few-point credit inquiry is a small cost.

Losing a house, overpaying, or discovering a credit issue too late is a much larger one.

## A Better Way to Think About the Credit Hit

Don’t ask:

**“How do I avoid any credit inquiry at all?”**

Ask:

**“How do I get a real answer with the least unnecessary credit activity?”**

That usually looks like this:

1. Review your own credit first.
2. Get a real preapproval with one lender who will actually underwrite the file.
3. If you want comparison quotes, shop other mortgage lenders in a short window.
4. Do not open new credit, finance furniture, or change jobs while the loan is in process.
5. Keep the file current until you close.

That process can include a hard inquiry.

It should not include a bunch of unrelated credit applications.

## So, Does Getting Preapproved for a Mortgage Hurt Your Credit?

It can, a little.

A real preapproval often includes a hard inquiry. That inquiry may lower your score by a few points for a period of time.

For most buyers, that is not the thing that hurts the purchase.

What helps the purchase is knowing:

**What you qualify for.**

**What you can comfortably afford.**

**Whether anything on your credit report needs to be cleaned up first.**

**Whether your offer will be taken seriously.**

Don’t avoid preapproval to protect a few credit points.

Protect your credit by avoiding new debt, late payments, and last-minute financial changes after you are approved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does getting preapproved for a mortgage hurt your credit?

It can cause a small, temporary drop if the lender places a hard inquiry. For most borrowers, that drop is only a few points and is much smaller than the impact of new loans, maxed-out credit cards, or late payments.

### Is a mortgage preapproval a hard inquiry or a soft pull?

It depends on the lender and the stage of the process. An estimate or prequalification may involve a soft pull or no pull. A full preapproval that includes underwriting typically requires a hard credit inquiry.

### How many points will a mortgage preapproval drop my credit score?

There is no fixed number. A single mortgage inquiry often changes a score by only a few points, though the exact effect depends on the borrower’s credit profile and the scoring model used.

### Do multiple mortgage preapprovals hurt my credit more?

Not necessarily. The CFPB notes that multiple mortgage credit checks within a 45-day window are generally recorded as a single inquiry. Some scoring models use a shorter shopping window.

### How long does a mortgage inquiry stay on my credit report?

Hard inquiries typically remain on a credit report for about two years. Their effect on your score is usually strongest at first and fades as the inquiry ages.

### Will my credit be pulled again before closing?

Usually, yes. Lenders commonly re-check credit before closing, which is why new debt, large credit-card balances, or late payments during the contract period can create problems.

### Should I get preapproved if I am not buying for several months?

A full preapproval is most useful when you are ready to shop. If you are a few months away, a credit and documentation review can still help identify issues while you have time to improve them.

### What hurts credit more than a preapproval?

New auto loans, furniture financing, high credit-card utilization, late payments, closing old accounts, and co-signing other debt can all affect your score and your mortgage qualification more than the original preapproval inquiry.
