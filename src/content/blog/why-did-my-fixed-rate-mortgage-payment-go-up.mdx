**Your total mortgage payment can increase even when your interest rate stays the same.** On a typical fully amortizing fixed-rate mortgage, the scheduled principal-and-interest payment stays level. But property taxes, homeowners insurance, and the amount collected for escrow can change.

Before assuming your lender changed your rate, or that you need to refinance, compare the pieces of your old and new payments.

## Fixed rate does not mean every housing cost is fixed

Your monthly payment to the mortgage company may include:

- Principal and interest.
- Money deposited into escrow for property taxes and homeowners insurance.
- Mortgage insurance, if applicable.
- Other amounts identified on your statement.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains the difference between principal and interest and your total payment](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/on-a-mortgage-whats-the-difference-between-my-principal-and-interest-payment-and-my-total-monthly-payment-en-1941/). If you have HOA or condo dues, those are usually paid separately.

An escrow account, sometimes called an impound account, holds money your servicer collects to pay covered property bills. Property taxes and insurance premiums can change from year to year, and when they do, the monthly escrow collection can change too. The [CFPB’s escrow overview](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-escrow-or-impound-account-en-140/) explains how it works.

## Why can an escrow increase feel like a double hit?

There can be two separate pieces:

**1. Higher costs going forward.** If the projected annual tax and insurance bills increase, the servicer may need to collect more each month for upcoming bills.

**2. An existing escrow shortage.** The account may also be below its required target balance. Repaying that shortage can add another amount to your payment.

A shortage does not necessarily mean you missed a mortgage payment. It means the escrow balance is below the target determined by the analysis. Bill amounts and payment timing affect that calculation.

For covered loans, [Regulation X](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/17/) requires an escrow analysis at the end of the escrow computation year. That is a 12-month period the servicer sets for your account, so it does not necessarily match the calendar year.

## A simple payment example

Suppose your statement previously showed:

| Payment component | Previous monthly amount | New monthly amount |
|---|---:|---:|
| Principal and interest | $2,000 | $2,000 |
| Ongoing tax and insurance collection | $650 | $800 |
| Escrow shortage repayment | $0 | $100 |
| Total | $2,650 | $2,900 |

In this illustration, projected annual tax and insurance costs rose by $1,800, adding $150 per month. Separately, an identified $1,200 shortage is being repaid over 12 months, adding $100.

Your total payment increased by $250, but your principal-and-interest payment did not change.

This is a simplified budgeting example, not a full escrow analysis. It assumes no mortgage insurance or other charges. The servicer’s actual calculation also considers bill timing, account balances, and any permitted cushion.

## Does a shortage have to be paid all at once?

Not necessarily. Regulation X limits how a servicer can handle a shortage for a borrower who is current (the servicer receives payments within 30 days of the due date):

- **Shortage of at least one month’s escrow payment:** the servicer can leave it outstanding or require repayment in equal monthly installments over at least 12 months.
- **Smaller shortage:** the servicer can leave it outstanding, require repayment within 30 days, or spread it in equal monthly installments over at least 12 months.

A negative escrow balance, called a deficiency, has different repayment rules. Do not assume every notice uses the same repayment schedule.

The [CFPB’s mortgage servicing FAQs](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/mortgage-resources/mortserv/mortgage-servicing-faqs/) explain these distinctions. They also note that a servicer may accept a lump-sum payment you choose to make toward a shortage, but can’t require one or offer it as an option on the annual escrow statement.

A voluntary shortage payment, if properly arranged and applied, does not eliminate higher tax or insurance costs going forward. Ask what your revised payment would actually be before sending extra money.

## What should I check first?

Gather your old and new mortgage statements, latest escrow analysis, property tax bill, and insurance renewal. Then ask your servicer:

1. Did my principal-and-interest payment change, or only escrow and other charges?
2. Which tax or insurance bill changed, and what amount did you use?
3. How much of the increase is ongoing collection versus shortage repayment?
4. What repayment period applies, and when is that portion scheduled to end?
5. Are the property information, bill amounts, and payment records correct?

If escrow does not explain the difference, check whether a temporary buydown ended, your mortgage insurance changed, another loan feature changed, or a fee was added. The [CFPB lists these and other possible reasons for payment changes](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/why-did-my-monthly-mortgage-payment-go-up-or-change-en-213/).

For homeowners in Texas or California, use the actual local tax records and insurance renewal, not a neighbor’s payment, to investigate the change. This article does not determine your property’s tax assessment or exemption eligibility.

## Quick questions

**Will my payment return to the old amount after the shortage is repaid?**

Not necessarily. The shortage-repayment portion may end, but ongoing tax and insurance costs may remain higher. Future analyses can change the amount again.

**Would removing escrow eliminate these costs?**

No. Even if your loan permits an escrow waiver, property taxes and required insurance still need to be paid. You would need to budget for those bills directly.

**Should I refinance because my payment increased?**

First identify the cause. Refinancing does not make property taxes or insurance disappear. Any new loan should be evaluated using its rate, closing costs, term, and total payment.

**What if the servicer made a mistake?**

Contact the servicer and keep records. If the issue is not resolved, the [CFPB explains](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/why-did-my-monthly-mortgage-payment-go-up-or-change-en-213/) that you can send a written notice of error to the servicer’s designated address, which may differ from its payment address. Do not assume a dispute suspends your payment obligations.
