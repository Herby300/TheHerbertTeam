One of the biggest misunderstandings I see around divorce and the marital home sounds something like this:

**“The divorce decree says I'm keeping the house, so my ex is off the mortgage now...right?”**

Not necessarily.

And this is exactly why I believe the mortgage conversation should happen **before the divorce agreement is finalized whenever possible.**

There are really three separate issues:

1. **Who owns the house?**
2. **Who owes the mortgage company?**
3. **What does the divorce agreement require each spouse to do?**

Those three things are related.

But they are not the same thing.

## A Divorce Decree Doesn't Rewrite Your Mortgage

Let's use a simple example.

John and Sarah bought a house together.

Both are borrowers on the mortgage.

During their divorce, they agree that Sarah will keep the home and be responsible for making the mortgage payments.

The divorce decree reflects that agreement.

From a divorce-planning standpoint, that may settle who gets the house and who is expected to make the payment.

But it doesn't automatically erase John's contractual obligation to the mortgage company.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-contact-me-about-a-debt-after-a-divorce-en-1413/) explains that a divorce decree or property settlement may assign responsibility for a joint debt to one spouse, but a creditor can generally still pursue anyone whose name remains on the loan agreement unless that person has been contractually released or the loan has been refinanced.

That's a very important distinction.

## Title and Mortgage Are Two Different Things

Here's another misconception:

**“I'll just sign the house over to my spouse and then I'll be off the mortgage.”**

That doesn't necessarily work either.

Being removed from **title** changes an ownership interest in the property.

Being removed from the **mortgage obligation** deals with responsibility for the debt.

The CFPB specifically notes that taking someone's name off the title to a home does not remove that person from the mortgage.

That's why I don't like relying on phrases such as:

**“We'll take care of the mortgage after the divorce.”**

I want to know what that actually means.

## Does the Spouse Keeping the House Have to Refinance?

Sometimes refinancing is the appropriate solution.

But here's another important point:

**Refinancing isn't automatically the only possible option.**

Depending on the existing mortgage and circumstances, there may potentially be an assumption or other servicing option available.

[Fannie Mae](https://yourhome.fanniemae.com/changing-or-transferring-ownership-home) explains that certain transfers resulting from divorce or separation can qualify as protected ownership transfers. It also explains that a spouse who receives the home may potentially assume an eligible existing mortgage and request a **release of liability** for the former spouse.

That's especially worth investigating when the existing mortgage has an attractive interest rate.

But an assumption and a release of liability are not the same thing as simply changing the title.

And they aren't available or appropriate in every situation.

The mortgage servicer and applicable loan guidelines matter.

## What Is a Release of Liability?

Think of this as the part that matters to the spouse leaving the home.

If both spouses originally borrowed the money, the departing spouse generally wants more than:

**“My ex promised to make the payments.”**

They want to know whether they have actually been released from responsibility for that mortgage.

Fannie Mae describes a release of liability as removing an existing borrower from the obligations of the mortgage loan. It also notes that an existing borrower generally won't be released unless the person taking responsibility for the mortgage qualifies after a financial and credit evaluation.

That's why mortgage feasibility matters.

If Sarah is supposed to keep the home, we need to determine whether Sarah can actually qualify for the solution contemplated by the divorce agreement.

## Why Does This Matter to the Spouse Leaving?

Imagine John is no longer living in the home.

Sarah owns it.

Sarah is supposed to make the payment.

But John's name is still on the mortgage.

Now Sarah misses payments.

That can become John's problem too.

The CFPB makes the underlying principle clear: divorce changes the relationship between spouses, but it doesn't automatically change their contractual relationship with creditors.

It can also create another practical problem.

John may want to buy another home.

If the existing mortgage remains in his name, we need to determine how that obligation will be treated when he applies for his next mortgage.

That doesn't automatically mean he can't qualify.

It means we need to know about it **before he starts shopping for another house.**

## Why Does This Matter to the Spouse Keeping the House?

Sarah has a different concern.

She may have agreed to refinance or otherwise remove John from the mortgage within a certain period.

But can she actually qualify?

We may need to evaluate:

- Her income after the divorce
- Existing debts
- Support being paid or received
- Credit
- The new mortgage amount
- Any equity buyout
- Property taxes and insurance
- The resulting monthly payment
- The requirements of the specific loan program

The worst time to discover that the proposed financing doesn't work is **after the agreement requiring it has already been signed.**

## This Is Where a [Mortgage Feasibility Review](/schedule) Helps

Before the final divorce agreement is completed, I like to model the proposed housing outcome.

If one spouse wants to keep the home, let's determine:

**Can that person qualify?**

**How much needs to be financed?**

**Is an equity buyout involved?**

**What happens to the existing mortgage?**

**Is refinancing the best route, or should an eligible assumption/release-of-liability option be investigated?**

**What will the new payment look like?**

**And what does the departing spouse need in order to move forward with their own housing plans?**

That isn't legal advice.

The attorneys determine how property and obligations should be addressed in the divorce.

My role is different:

**I help determine whether the proposed mortgage strategy is actually financeable.**

## A Note for Divorce Attorneys and Mediators

This is one area where involving a mortgage professional earlier can potentially prevent a difficult problem later.

If a proposed settlement says something like:

**“Spouse A shall refinance the marital residence and remove Spouse B from the mortgage within X days...”**

there is a mortgage question worth answering before that obligation is finalized:

**Can Spouse A actually qualify to do it?**

And is refinancing even the most appropriate available mortgage solution?

## Plan the Mortgage Before You Finalize the House Decision

If you're going through a divorce and one spouse wants to keep the home, don't assume that awarding the house, changing title, and removing someone from the mortgage are all the same event.

They're not.

Figure out the mortgage path early.

It can make the decisions around the marital home much clearer for both spouses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a divorce decree remove my ex-spouse from the mortgage?

Not automatically. A divorce decree can allocate responsibility for a debt between former spouses, but it does not itself rewrite the mortgage contract or automatically release a borrower from liability to the creditor.

### If my ex signs over the house, are they off the mortgage?

Not necessarily. Removing someone from title and removing someone from mortgage liability are separate issues.

### Do I always have to refinance after divorce to remove my ex from the mortgage?

Not always. Depending on the existing loan and circumstances, an eligible mortgage assumption and release of liability may be another possible path. The mortgage servicer should be contacted to determine what options are available.

### What is a mortgage release of liability?

It is the release of an existing borrower from obligations under the mortgage loan. Qualification and servicing requirements can apply.

### Should we figure this out before the divorce is final?

When possible, that's generally when I prefer to evaluate the mortgage feasibility. It allows the client and their attorney to understand whether the contemplated financing strategy appears workable before relying on it in the final housing plan.
