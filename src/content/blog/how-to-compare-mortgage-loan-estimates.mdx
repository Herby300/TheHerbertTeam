Two mortgage offers can sound similar on the phone and feel very different when you put the paperwork on the table. Before choosing an offer, give yourself time to understand what you are comparing and what you still need explained.

A helpful starting question is: “Do these offers describe the same financing plan?” From there, you can build a short list of questions instead of trying to make a decision from one headline number.

> **Quick answer:** Compare Loan Estimates using consistent assumptions. Review the loan structure, payment, upfront expenses, and cash needs together, and ask the lender to explain differences before choosing an offer.

## Start with comparable requests

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends asking each lender for the same kind of loan with the same features. It also advises checking whether the quoted rate is locked, because some lenders lock the rate when they issue the Loan Estimate and some don't. An unlocked rate can change. A lender generally must send a Loan Estimate within three business days after receiving six pieces of information: your name, income, Social Security number, the property address, an estimated property value, and the loan amount. You do not need a signed purchase contract to request one. [CFPB: requesting Loan Estimates](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/request-and-review-multiple-loan-estimates/)

For your own review, write a one-sentence description of the financing you want evaluated. Send the same description when asking each lender about options. If a lender proposes another structure, keep it as a separate scenario and ask why it might suit your situation.

Make your worksheet practical. Add the date of each conversation, the person who answered your questions, and anything you still need confirmed. Your notes should help you follow up; they should not replace the official documents.

## Read the monthly payment in context

On the Loan Estimate, principal and interest are only part of the payment picture. Review the projected payment and the taxes, insurance, and assessments, including amounts identified as not escrowed, which you would pay directly. Page one also identifies important features such as the loan product and term, whether the payment can increase, and whether a balloon payment or prepayment penalty applies. The document's estimated cash to close is a different figure from its estimated closing costs. [CFPB: Loan Estimate Explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/loan-estimate/)

For your household budget, place the proposed housing expense beside utilities, maintenance, transportation, childcare, and savings. This is your own planning exercise, not a mortgage qualification calculation.

Ask yourself what you want available after the move. Would a repair or an unexpected expense leave enough room in the budget? A lender conversation can help you understand financing, while your own spending plan helps you decide what feels comfortable.

## Look at the upfront costs and credits

The CFPB suggests comparing lender-related charges and credits, including origination charges in Section A, services in Section B, and lender credits in Section J. It also describes a five-year comparison: subtract the principal paid off from the total paid in the “In 5 Years” section on page three to see the cost of borrowing over that period. That period may differ from how long you actually keep the loan. [CFPB: comparing offers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/compare-loan-estimates/)

Use the form to organize the discussion rather than treating a single total as the answer. Ask what explains each meaningful difference and which assumptions remain estimates.

A useful follow-up is, “Can you walk me through the difference between these two offers using the same scenario?” If the explanation changes your understanding, update your notes and ask whether the written estimate also needs clarification.

## Understand the role of APR

The interest rate and annual percentage rate are different measures. APR reflects the interest rate plus certain credit costs, including items such as points and mortgage insurance. The Federal Trade Commission recommends comparing rates and fees from several lenders or brokers rather than relying on the payment or interest rate alone. [FTC: mortgage shopping](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/shopping-mortgage-faqs)

Bring questions about unfamiliar terms to the loan officer instead of guessing what they mean. Ask how the figures relate to the specific offer in front of you. Keep the explanation alongside the document so you can revisit it before deciding. If one offer includes points, see [Should I Pay Points to Lower My Mortgage Rate?](/blog/should-i-pay-points-to-lower-mortgage-rate/)

## A fictional comparison conversation

Imagine that Alex has two offers and is drawn to the one with the more attractive headline. Instead of selecting it immediately, Alex prepares a discussion sheet:

| My question | What I want explained |
| --- | --- |
| Are these the same scenario? | Any difference in the requested financing |
| What will my budget look like? | The housing expense I should plan around |
| What do I need upfront? | The money needed for this transaction |
| What remains uncertain? | Assumptions that need further confirmation |
| What happens next? | Review milestones and responsibilities |

Alex then asks each lender to explain the offer in writing and describe the next steps. The point of the exercise is better understanding. It does not establish that either loan is available, affordable, or approved.

Alex is fictional. No rate, lender recommendation, or borrower outcome is represented.

## Keep a decision record

As a personal planning aid, create a short page headed “What I understand” and another headed “What I need answered.” Add questions as you read. Cross them off only when you understand the response.

Consider including:

- The financing scenario you asked each lender to evaluate.
- The date of the written offer you are reviewing.
- Questions about unfamiliar figures or features.
- Your target closing timeline.
- The next document or explanation you are expecting.
- The cash cushion you want to preserve after moving.

Keep personal loan paperwork in a secure location. Share it through a process arranged with the professional reviewing it. Public comments and social media messages are not appropriate places for account details or application information.

## Questions to ask before choosing

You do not need to sound like an underwriter to have a productive conversation. Plain questions work well:

- “Which parts of this offer should I look at first?”
- “What assumptions are different between these options?”
- “What would you like me to understand about this loan structure?”
- “What is still subject to review?”
- “Who should I contact if a figure does not match what we discussed?”

Write down the answers in your own words. If you cannot explain the answer back simply, ask for another explanation. A good decision process gives you a clear understanding of the proposal and the next steps.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to choose from a rate advertisement?

Use an advertisement as a starting point for questions. Request written information about the financing you actually want reviewed. This article does not evaluate any advertised offer or recommend a lender.

### Can someone help me organize the comparison?

Ask a mortgage professional to walk through the offers and explain the assumptions. Keep the documents and your notes together so the discussion stays grounded in the actual proposals.

### Does this comparison mean I am approved?

No. Comparing offers is part of understanding your options. Individual eligibility and terms require review of the full application, property, and applicable loan requirements.

### What if I am still early in the home search?

Start by describing your goals and asking what information a lender will need. Be clear about which details are known and which are still hypothetical.

### What is one useful step I can take today?

Write down the housing decision you are trying to make and the questions you need answered. Bring that list to a mortgage conversation before making commitments based on assumptions.
