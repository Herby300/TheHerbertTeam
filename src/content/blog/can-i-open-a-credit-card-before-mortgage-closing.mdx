You are getting ready to move, and a store offers a discount if you apply for its credit card. Or you are considering a rewards card for upcoming purchases. The offer may be tempting, especially when there is a long list of things you want for the new home.

Before applying, talk with your mortgage team.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau advises avoiding applications for other credit right before or during the mortgage process because an additional inquiry can lower credit scores. That is a reason to pause and ask questions, not a prediction that every application will cause a mortgage denial. [CFPB: mortgage credit checks](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-exactly-happens-when-a-mortgage-lender-checks-my-credit-en-2005/)

> **Quick answer:** Contact your mortgage team before applying for a new credit card while your home loan is in process. If you already applied, tell the team what happened so the actual change can be reviewed.

## Why the conversation matters before closing

The CFPB explains that credit checks can occur when you apply for a loan and just before closing. The timing and review for your particular file should be discussed with your lender. Do not base a purchase decision on an assumption that the initial credit review was the final one. [CFPB: when lenders check credit](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/when-will-a-lender-run-a-credit-check-or-obtain-a-copy-of-my-credit-report-en-322/)

A useful question is, “What changes should I tell you about while my application is in progress?” Ask this early so you know who to contact if something comes up.

Keep that person's contact information alongside your closing checklist. When you have a question about a purchase, it is easier to send a clear message than to reconstruct the details later. For other changes to avoid while your loan is in process, see [Preapproved for a Mortgage? Avoid These 9 Mistakes Before Closing](/blog/preapproved-mortgage-mistakes-before-closing/).

## Additional debt can require a fresh calculation

For Fannie Mae loans, when additional liabilities are disclosed or discovered after the underwriting decision and through closing, the lender must recalculate the debt-to-income ratio. If a current debt appears on the credit report but not on the loan application, the guide says the borrower should provide a reasonable explanation, and documentation may be required to support it. This is a specific conventional-loan rule, not a statement that every program uses identical requirements. [Fannie Mae: General Information on Liabilities](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-01/general-information-liabilities)

Debt-to-income is a comparison of monthly debt obligations with qualifying monthly income. As an arithmetic illustration, suppose a worksheet has $6,000 of qualifying monthly income and $2,400 of monthly debt obligations. That is 40%. If an additional counted payment were $150, the same arithmetic would produce 42.5%.

This fictional calculation is not a qualifying limit or an underwriting result. It does not establish how a lender would treat a particular card. The point is that you should ask for a file-specific review instead of deciding that a payment is too small to matter.

## What to send if you already applied

Start with a factual note. You can use a message like this:

> “I applied for a credit account on [date]. The creditor is [name]. The application is [pending/approved/declined]. I have [made/not made] a purchase. Please tell me what information you need and how to provide it securely.”

Then make a list of the records you have. That might include the account approval, purchase receipt, current balance information, and payment terms. Ask which records are actually needed before sending sensitive material.

Do not change a document to make it easier to explain. If something is unclear, say so and ask what will help resolve it. Keep the original records and a copy of your correspondence.

This is a preparation checklist, not a universal underwriting document requirement. The lender should identify what is needed for your actual transaction.

## Do not try to solve it by guessing

If you are worried about a recent application, ask the mortgage team what to do next. Avoid making a series of account changes based on advice intended for someone else's situation.

A helpful follow-up question is, “Before I take another step, what should be reviewed?” Write down the answer and the person responsible for the follow-up. Ask what remains unresolved and when you should check back.

You do not need to diagnose the credit file yourself. Your task is to give accurate information and understand the next steps.

## A fictional furniture-shopping example

Taylor is buying a home and wants furniture delivered after the move. At the store, Taylor is offered a new financing account.

Before accepting, Taylor writes down the purchase price, proposed terms, and delivery timing. Taylor asks the mortgage team to review the planned transaction before an application is submitted.

If the purchase can wait, Taylor can ask the store about holding the order without making a new credit commitment. If the purchase is urgent, Taylor can explain the urgency and ask the mortgage team what needs to be evaluated.

This scenario does not promise that delaying, financing, or paying another way will work. It shows how to bring a real decision to the right conversation before committing.

Taylor is fictional. No retailer, card product, or loan outcome is recommended.

## Build a short purchase plan for the move

Separate the items you need immediately from those you would simply like to have. Discuss the list with anyone sharing the household budget.

| Planning question | Why write it down? |
| --- | --- |
| What is needed for move-in? | Helps establish your priorities |
| What could wait? | Creates choices about timing |
| What does the seller require today? | Clarifies the decision in front of you |
| What do I need explained? | Gives the mortgage team a concrete question |
| Who is following up? | Keeps the next step clear |

This table is a personal planning tool. It is not an approval worksheet and does not replace the lender's analysis.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is opening a card an automatic mortgage denial?
This article cannot determine your result. Explain the actual change to the mortgage team and ask what review is needed. Avoid assuming either that the application has no consequence or that the home purchase is already lost.

### What if I applied but did not use the card?
Tell the lender that precise sequence. A pending application, an opened account, and a purchase are different facts to report. Ask what documentation is needed rather than leaving the team to guess.

### What if the offer says there are no payments right away?
Give the mortgage team the actual offer terms and ask how they would be reviewed. A marketing headline is not an individualized mortgage decision.

### Will my lender check credit again?
The CFPB identifies a preclosing check as one possible point of review. Ask your lender about your file; do not rely on a promised universal schedule.

### Should I post my account details in a social media message?
No. Ask for the professional's secure document process. Public comments are appropriate for general questions, not account numbers, financial statements, or application details.

### What is the first step if I am unsure?
Contact your mortgage team before committing. Describe the purchase or application accurately and ask what needs to happen next.
