**Yes, your parent may be able to help you qualify for a mortgage without living in the home.** On some loans, they can apply with you as a *non-occupant borrower*. A lender may then consider their qualifying income along with yours.

That does not mean the lender simply adds your parent's salary to your application. Their existing housing payment, other debts, credit, and assets also enter the review. And if they sign the mortgage note, they take on responsibility for repayment—even if you plan to make every payment yourself.

## How does a non-occupant borrower work?

A non-occupant borrower applies for the loan but will not live in the home as their primary residence. Under [Fannie Mae's conventional-loan guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-2-04/guarantors-co-signers-or-non-occupant-borrowers-subject-transaction), that person may or may not have an ownership interest on the property title. They sign the mortgage note and share liability for the loan.

You may also hear the term *co-signer*. Loan programs can use these terms differently, particularly when describing whether the person will be on title. Before applying, ask the lender exactly how your parent would be listed **on the loan and on the title**.

## Will adding my parent's income improve my approval?

It might. Fannie Mae says qualifying income from a non-occupant borrower may be considered if it meets the same income standards that apply to an occupying borrower. But its automated underwriting system also evaluates **all borrowers' income, assets, liabilities, and credit**. The lender includes the parent's own housing expense and applicable debts when calculating the combined debt-to-income ratio.

For example, imagine your qualifying monthly income is **$5,000**. Your proposed total housing payment is **$2,200**, and your other monthly debts are **$400**. Your example debt-to-income ratio would be **52%**. That is above the 50% maximum Fannie Mae generally allows for loans underwritten through its Desktop Underwriter system.

Now imagine your parent has **$6,000** in qualifying monthly income and **$2,000** in housing and other monthly obligations. Together, the example would be **$4,600 in monthly obligations divided by $11,000 in qualifying income**, or about **41.8%**.

That comparison shows why a parent's income could help. It does **not** establish loan eligibility. The lender must verify both applicants' information and evaluate the loan under the applicable program, underwriting method, property, credit, and down-payment rules. See [Fannie Mae's debt-to-income guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-02/debt-income-ratios) and [non-occupant borrower income guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.1-01/general-income-information).

## Does adding a parent change the down payment?

It can. Under Fannie Mae's guidance, a loan with a non-occupant borrower submitted through Desktop Underwriter generally cannot exceed **95% loan-to-value** unless an allowed Community Seconds arrangement applies. That generally means planning for at least **5% down** on a purchase under this specific conventional structure, subject to the full loan details.

Manually underwritten loans have different limits and additional rules concerning the occupying borrower's contribution and debt-to-income ratio. Other loan programs may handle non-occupant borrowers differently. **Do not assume that the down-payment option you saw advertised without a parent will still apply after adding one.** Have your lender compare both structures before you make an offer.

## What is the risk to my parent?

A co-borrower or co-signer is agreeing to repay the mortgage with you. If payments are missed, their credit and finances can be affected. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/mortgages/answers/key-terms/) explains that this responsibility can extend to the full loan amount.

You should also discuss whether your parent would have an ownership interest. **Being on the mortgage and being on the title are separate questions.** Title affects property rights, and those rights can depend on state law and how the property is titled. If ownership is part of the plan, a Texas or California real estate attorney can help your family understand the proposed arrangement before closing.

Finally, do not assume your parent can simply be removed from the mortgage after you have made a few payments. Ask the lender what would actually be required to release them from the loan later. Depending on the loan and circumstances, you may need to qualify on your own for a new loan or meet another available release process.

## Questions to ask before applying together

1. Do I qualify on my own, and how do the payment and cash needed at closing compare?
2. Which loan programs allow my parent to help without occupying the home?
3. How will my parent's income, housing payment, debts, and credit be counted?
4. What down payment would this specific loan structure require?
5. Would my parent be on the mortgage, the title, or both?
6. What would it take to remove my parent from the loan later?

## Quick questions

### Does my parent have to live in the house?

Not necessarily. Fannie Mae allows a qualifying non-occupant borrower on certain conventional loans.

### Does my parent have to be on the deed?

Fannie Mae's definition of a non-occupant borrower allows the person to be on title or off title. Your lender and closing team must confirm how the specific transaction will be structured.

### Is a non-occupant borrower the same as a co-signer?

Not always. Both sign the note and share responsibility for the loan. Some programs use "co-signer" to mean someone who is not on the title and has no ownership interest, while a non-occupant borrower may or may not be on title. Ask your lender which term applies to your loan.

### Can my parent just give me money instead?

Possibly. A [documented gift](/blog/how-much-money-do-i-really-need-to-buy-a-house#gift-funds) is a different option from joining the mortgage. For a one-unit primary residence, Fannie Mae generally allows the entire down payment to come from an eligible family gift, documented with a signed gift letter. The source of the funds, donor, property type, and loan program matter. Ask your lender to compare a gift with a non-occupant borrower structure. See [Fannie Mae's personal gift rules](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-04/personal-gifts).

A parent's help can be valuable, but there is more than one way to structure it.
