When you plan to buy a home, it is natural to focus on the down payment. But another question deserves attention before you commit your savings: what money will still be available after the purchase?

Start by separating the lender's review from your household plan. Ask what the loan requires, then consider what you want available for life after the move.

> **Quick answer:** Mortgage reserves are qualifying assets available after closing. Whether a reserve requirement applies, how much is needed, and what assets count depend on the actual financing review. Your personal emergency savings goal is a separate planning decision.

## What does “reserves” mean in a mortgage review?

Fannie Mae defines financial reserves as liquid or near-liquid assets available to a borrower after the mortgage closes. Funds needed to close are subtracted when assessing reserves. Its guide measures reserves in months of the qualifying payment for the new mortgage, and minimum requirements vary with the transaction, the occupancy and amortization type of the property, the number of units, and the number of other financed properties the borrower owns. For loans underwritten through Fannie Mae's automated system, that system determines the requirement. These are Fannie Mae conventional-loan rules, not a universal rule for every mortgage program. [Fannie Mae: reserve requirements](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-01/minimum-reserve-requirements)

Ask the lender to explain your specific review. A useful question is, “What reserve requirement are you evaluating for this loan, and which records do you need?” Write down the answer instead of relying on an amount someone else needed for a different purchase.

## Keep three planning amounts separate

Use a page with three headings:

| Heading | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Money needed for the transaction | What does the current financing estimate say I must provide? |
| Assets the lender can count afterward | What remains eligible under the applicable loan review? |
| My household cushion | What do I want available for expenses and surprises? |

These headings help organize the conversation. They are not three accounts you must open, and this table is not an underwriting worksheet.

For example, you might want to ask whether a balance on your personal worksheet is being counted for the loan review or simply listed as part of your own planning. Clarifying that distinction early can prevent you from treating every figure as interchangeable.

## A fictional example of money remaining

Imagine that Casey's personal planning sheet begins with $45,000 of available savings. The sheet sets aside $30,000 for the proposed transaction and $3,000 for planned moving expenses. The arithmetic leaves $12,000.

That $12,000 is a household planning result, not proof of qualifying reserves or an approval. The lender still needs to evaluate the actual transaction, funds, records, and applicable requirements.

Casey also needs to decide whether that remaining amount fits the household's needs. A number that looks comfortable on a simple sheet may feel different after considering the first months in the new home.

Casey and all amounts are fictional. No loan, savings target, or borrower outcome is recommended.

## Plan the upfront contribution with the whole move in mind

The CFPB suggests considering moving costs, renovations, other savings goals, and an emergency cushion when deciding how much cash is available for a home purchase. It also reminds buyers to account for closing costs in addition to the down payment. [CFPB: planning your down payment](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/prepare/determine-your-down-payment/)

Before selecting a contribution amount, write down the expenses you already expect. Separate a necessary moving expense from an optional purchase that could wait. Ask the mortgage team to explain the financing scenarios you want reviewed, rather than assuming one contribution amount is automatically best.

If you are buying with another person, discuss this worksheet together. Agree on which questions need answers and who will follow up. This is a planning exercise, not a substitute for an individualized financing review.

## Your emergency savings goal has its own purpose

The CFPB describes an emergency fund as money set aside for unplanned expenses such as a home repair, medical bill, or loss of income. Its guidance says the amount depends on your circumstances and suggests thinking about unexpected expenses you have experienced before. [CFPB: emergency savings](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/)

For your own discussion, ask: what would be difficult to pay for immediately after moving? What expenses are predictable, and what would be a surprise? How would you rebuild savings after using some of them?

You do not need to answer those questions with someone else's preferred formula. A household plan should reflect the decision you are actually trying to make and the resources you can realistically maintain. For more on weighing a larger down payment against cash on hand, see [How Much Money Should You Have Left After Buying a House?](/blog/how-much-money-left-after-buying-house/)

## Gather records before moving money

Start with the records you already have and request the lender's secure document checklist. Make a list of the accounts and assets you want reviewed, without assuming all of them will be accepted.

If you are considering a transfer, sale, withdrawal, gift, or another change, describe the plan before acting. Ask what information the lender needs and bring tax or legal questions to an appropriate professional. This article does not recommend withdrawing retirement funds or rearranging assets to qualify. For why lenders ask about recent deposits, see [Why Does My Mortgage Lender Ask About Bank Deposits?](/blog/why-does-my-mortgage-lender-ask-about-bank-deposits/)

Keep original records and a simple note of what you provided. Avoid sending account statements through public comments or social messages. Use the delivery process arranged with the professional handling the review.

## Questions to bring to your mortgage conversation

- What amount needs to be available for closing in the scenario we are reviewing?
- Is a reserve requirement being applied to this file?
- Which assets and records are being evaluated?
- What assumptions remain unverified?
- How should I report a planned change before moving funds?
- What should be updated if my purchase plans change?

Ask for explanations in plain language. If a figure on your worksheet does not match the lender's figures, ask what accounts for the difference.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every buyer need the same reserve amount?
Do not use another buyer's requirement as your answer. Ask the lender to identify what applies to your loan and transaction.

### Is the money left in my account automatically qualifying reserves?
A leftover balance on a personal worksheet is not an eligibility decision. Ask the mortgage team to evaluate the actual records and explain what is being counted.

### Does meeting a loan requirement tell me how much emergency savings I need?
Treat those as separate questions. An individual financing review and your own household spending plan serve different purposes.

### Should I put every available dollar toward the down payment?
This article does not recommend a contribution amount. Compare the scenarios with your lender and consider your household plan before committing funds.

### What can I do before I choose a house?
Organize your records, describe your purchase goals, and ask what information a lender would need for an initial review. Be clear about which figures are known and which are hypothetical.
