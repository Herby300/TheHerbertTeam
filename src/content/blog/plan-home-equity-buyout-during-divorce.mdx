**Quick answer: Plan a home-equity buyout by separating the legal settlement calculation from the mortgage review. Have your attorney clarify the proposed agreement, and ask your mortgage team to evaluate the financing, cash needs, and timeline before you make commitments that depend on approval.**

If you hope to keep the family home after divorce, you may need a way to compensate your spouse for an agreed interest in the property. Before you commit to an amount and a deadline, bring the legal and financing questions into the same planning process.

Start with three separate questions:

- What amount does the proposed agreement require?
- How could that amount be paid?
- What housing budget would you have afterward?

## Begin with a worksheet, not a promise

A simple equity worksheet can help you organize the first discussion. For example, suppose a fictional home value is $400,000 and the mortgage balance used in a preliminary worksheet is $250,000. The difference is $150,000.

If the example assumes an equal split of that difference, the starting figure for each person is $75,000.

That arithmetic does not establish what either spouse is legally owed. It does not settle questions about ownership interests, other liens, credits, expenses, valuation, or the terms of an actual agreement. It also does not show whether financing is available.

Label the worksheet's assumptions clearly. Ask your attorney which figures and adjustments belong in your legal settlement analysis. Ask your mortgage professional which figures need separate verification for a financing review.

## The buyout amount and the financing request are different

Use two columns in your planning notes. In one, record the settlement proposal you are discussing with counsel. In the other, record the financing questions you want the lender to evaluate.

For a refinance scenario, the planning exercise might begin with an existing mortgage payoff, the proposed payment to the other owner, and estimated transaction expenses. Your lender should explain the actual loan request, any borrower cash contribution, and which items can be financed under the selected program.

Do not treat a rough total as an approved loan amount. Ask what remains unverified and what documents are needed to move from a conversation to a meaningful review.

This distinction is useful when you are discussing a deadline. You want to understand the financing assumptions before relying on a date that has legal or practical consequences.

## Is every buyout refinance a cash-out refinance?

No. Fannie Mae's limited cash-out guidance includes a specific co-owner buyout provision. It requires at least 12 months of joint ownership before the new loan's disbursement date, a written agreement signed by all parties that states the terms of the property transfer and the proposed disposition of the refinance proceeds, and that the person buying out the other owner's interest qualify for the mortgage. The owner who acquires sole ownership may not receive any of the refinance proceeds under this provision. The guide has an inheritance-related exception to the joint-ownership documentation requirement. A lender must evaluate the entire transaction, not just its label. [Fannie Mae: limited cash-out refinance transactions](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.3-02/limited-cash-out-refinance-transactions)

This is a Fannie Mae conventional-loan example. It does not establish identical treatment for Freddie Mac, FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, portfolio, or non-QM loans. Ask which program is being considered and what requirements apply to your facts.

In Texas, an owelty lien is one legal structure sometimes used for an equity buyout. See [What Is an Owelty Lien in a Texas Divorce?](/blog/texas-divorce-owelty-lien-equity-buyout/)

## Does changing the deed resolve the mortgage?

Ownership paperwork and responsibility to a creditor need separate attention. The CFPB explains that a divorce settlement does not automatically change the creditor relationship, and removing a name from title does not remove it from the mortgage. State law and the original loan agreement matter. [CFPB: debt after divorce](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-contact-me-about-a-debt-after-a-divorce-en-1413/)

Before signing transfer documents, ask your attorney how they fit the settlement and ask the mortgage professional or servicer how the existing debt will be handled. Keep those answers together so you can identify anything still unresolved. For more on this question, see [Does a Divorce Decree Remove Your Ex From the Mortgage?](/blog/divorce-decree-remove-ex-from-mortgage/)

A useful written question is: "What will document that the intended ownership transfer, payment, and mortgage arrangement have actually been completed?"

## Could keeping the existing mortgage be an option?

Ask the current mortgage servicer about options for the actual loan. Fannie Mae's consumer guidance explains that certain protected ownership transfers, including a transfer to a former spouse in a divorce or separation, may not require immediate repayment and refinancing. It also distinguishes an assumption from a release of an existing borrower's liability: an existing borrower is not released by the servicer unless the person taking over the loan qualifies. [Fannie Mae: changing ownership](https://yourhome.fanniemae.com/changing-or-transferring-ownership-home)

A proposed buyout still needs its own payment plan. Ask separately how the payment to your spouse would be funded and how any proposed arrangement fits the loan and settlement. Do not assume that an ownership-transfer option supplies the money for the buyout.

## Test the budget after the transaction

A practical household budget should sit beside the financing conversation. List the housing payment you are evaluating, taxes and insurance, utilities, maintenance, transportation, childcare, and other expenses you expect to handle.

Use your own spending records rather than someone else's suggested percentage. Write down how much cash you would like to keep available after the transaction and what expenses are likely in the first year.

As a personal planning exercise, ask how you would handle an urgent repair or a temporary interruption in expected cash flow. This is not an underwriting formula. It is a way to see whether the housing plan feels sustainable beyond the settlement date. Our post [Can I Keep the House After Divorce?](/blog/keep-house-after-divorce/) covers more of the affordability side.

## Gather questions and records before setting the timeline

Ask for a secure document checklist. Depending on what is being evaluated, you may already have mortgage statements, information about other liens, ownership records, income records, and draft or signed settlement documents available for professional review.

Do not send personal records through public comments or social messages. Confirm the delivery process with the professional handling your file.

Use this checklist to organize the conversation:

| Planning question | Person or team to ask |
| --- | --- |
| What does the proposed settlement require? | Your attorney |
| What valuation assumptions need clarification? | Your attorney and appropriate valuation professional |
| What financing scenario can be evaluated? | Your mortgage team |
| What options apply to the existing loan? | Your mortgage servicer |
| What must happen before money or ownership changes? | Your attorney and closing professionals |
| What does the plan mean for my taxes? | Your tax professional |

The table is a discussion aid, not a legal workflow or a complete closing checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is my spouse always entitled to half the equity?

The equal split above is a fictional assumption for arithmetic only. Have your attorney explain the actual ownership and settlement questions for your situation.

### Can I use an online value to finalize the buyout?

Treat any preliminary figure as an assumption until the relevant professionals explain what valuation is appropriate for their review. The legal settlement analysis and financing review may require different information.

### Does having equity mean I will qualify?

This article cannot establish eligibility. Ask for an individual review of the proposed transaction and the information needed to evaluate it.

### What if the refinance is not feasible by the proposed deadline?

Tell your attorney what remains unresolved before relying on that deadline. Discuss alternatives with the professionals reviewing your situation instead of assuming another option will be approved.

### Should I transfer title first?

Ask your attorney and mortgage or closing professionals to explain the sequence for your transaction before signing. This article does not recommend a universal transfer sequence.

### What should I do first?

Write down the proposed buyout, the assumptions behind it, and the timing you are considering. Bring those questions to your attorney and mortgage team before making commitments.
