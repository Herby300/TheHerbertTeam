The question often comes up shortly after a divorce agreement starts taking shape:

**“I’ll be receiving child support or alimony. Can I use that income to qualify for a mortgage?”**

The answer may be yes.

But receiving support and being able to use it as qualifying mortgage income are not automatically the same thing.

The lender generally needs to answer several questions:

- Is the payment documented?
- Have you actually been receiving it?
- Has it been paid consistently?
- How long will it continue?
- Does the applicable loan program allow it to be used?
- Do you want the income considered in your application?

There is also another side to this conversation.

If you are the spouse **paying** child support, alimony, or maintenance, that obligation may affect how much mortgage you can qualify for.

That is why support should be part of the mortgage-planning conversation before the divorce terms are finalized whenever possible.

## You Are Not Required to Use Support Income

Let’s begin with something important.

You generally [do not have to disclose](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/5305.1) child support, alimony, or separate maintenance income unless you want it considered when qualifying for the mortgage.

If your employment and other eligible income are sufficient, you may be able to qualify without relying on support.

If you need or want the support included, however, it must meet the applicable loan-program requirements.

For example, current [Fannie Mae guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.4-02/alimony-child-support-equalization-payments-or-separate-maintenance) says the income may be included when the borrower discloses it on the mortgage application and requests that it be considered.

## A Divorce Decree Alone May Not Be Enough

Imagine Sarah’s divorce decree says she will receive $2,000 per month in child support.

Sarah wants to use that $2,000 to qualify for her next home.

The decree gives us important information, including:

- The required payment
- Who must make it
- When payments begin
- How long payments are scheduled to continue
- The children or circumstances connected to the obligation

But the document by itself may not establish an acceptable payment history.

Under Fannie Mae’s current conventional guidance, the lender generally must document receipt of the income for the most recent six months. The payment history should show full, regular, and timely payments.

That history may be documented with items such as:

- Bank statements
- Canceled checks
- Electronic deposits
- Other acceptable evidence of receipt

This creates an important timing issue.

If the divorce agreement says payments begin next month, Sarah may not immediately have the receipt history required to use that income under a particular program.

That does not necessarily mean Sarah cannot obtain a mortgage.

It means we may need to determine whether she can qualify without that income, wait until an acceptable history is established, or evaluate another eligible loan strategy.

## The Income Must Be Expected to Continue

Payment history is only one part of the analysis.

The lender also needs to determine how long the support is expected to continue.

For Fannie Mae conventional financing, qualifying child support, alimony, equalization payments, or separate maintenance income generally must be expected to continue for at least three years from the mortgage note date.

For child support, the age of the child may therefore matter.

Suppose Sarah receives $2,000 per month for two children, but $1,000 of that payment will end when the older child reaches a specified age in eighteen months.

We should not simply assume the entire $2,000 will count throughout the mortgage qualification.

The lender must review the actual terms and determine what portion, if any, satisfies the required continuance period.

This is one reason the details of the agreement matter.

## Irregular Payments Can Change the Calculation

A divorce decree may require $2,000 per month.

But what if the payment record looks like this?

- $2,000 one month
- $1,500 the next month
- No payment the following month
- $3,000 paid later to catch up

The required amount and the amount that can actually be used for mortgage qualification may not be the same.

Late, partial, inconsistent, or recently started payments can affect whether the income is considered stable and how the lender calculates it.

A history of regular electronic transfers is generally much easier to document than payments made unpredictably or in cash.

If you expect to use support income for a future mortgage, establish a clear, traceable payment record.

## Child Support May Receive Different Tax Treatment

Child support is generally treated as nontaxable income to the recipient.

Certain mortgage programs may allow qualifying nontaxable income to be increased—or “grossed up”—when calculating qualifying income.

That does not mean the borrower receives more money.

It is an underwriting calculation intended to recognize that the qualifying income is not reduced by income taxes in the same way as taxable earnings.

Whether a gross-up is allowed, and the amount used, [depends on the loan program](https://www.hud.gov/hud-partners/single-family-handbook-4000-1) and underwriting requirements.

It should be treated as part of the mortgage analysis, not assumed in advance.

## Property-Settlement Payments Are Not Automatically Support Income

Divorce agreements may include several different types of payments.

These might include:

- Child support
- Alimony or spousal maintenance
- An equity buyout
- Equalization payments
- A lump-sum property settlement
- Reimbursement of particular expenses

Those payments are not necessarily treated the same way for mortgage qualification.

For example, Fannie Mae’s current guidance specifically notes that lump-sum equalization payments are not considered a steady source of qualifying income.

The label used in a conversation is not enough.

The lender needs to review the actual legal documentation, payment structure, history, and applicable loan guidelines.

This is also why clients should consult their attorneys and tax professionals about the legal and tax characterization of payments.

## What If You Are Paying Support?

Now let’s look at the other spouse.

Suppose John is required to pay:

- $1,500 per month in child support
- $1,000 per month in alimony

John wants to buy another home after the divorce.

Those obligations may affect his mortgage qualification.

Under current [Fannie Mae monthly-debt guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations), required child support, alimony, equalization, or separate-maintenance payments generally must be considered as recurring obligations when more than ten months of payments remain.

For certain Fannie Mae conventional files, alimony, equalization, and separate-maintenance obligations may sometimes be handled by reducing qualifying income instead of including the amount as a monthly debt.

That optional treatment does not extend to child support in the same way.

The exact impact depends on:

- The loan program
- The type of obligation
- The amount
- The remaining payment period
- The borrower’s other income and debts
- The underwriting system and lender requirements

The practical takeaway is simple:

**Support being paid should be included in the mortgage feasibility analysis from the beginning.**

Do not wait until you are under contract on another house to determine how it affects qualification.

## Can the Agreement Be Written to Help Mortgage Qualification?

The attorney—not the lender—determines the appropriate legal language and structure.

A mortgage professional should not tell the parties or attorneys how support must be characterized or negotiated.

What I can do is identify potential mortgage consequences.

For example:

- When are payments expected to begin?
- Will enough payment history exist before the planned home purchase?
- How long will each payment continue?
- Does the proposed buyer need the income to qualify?
- Can the buyer qualify without waiting to use support?
- How will the paying spouse’s obligation affect their future qualification?
- Are property-settlement payments being mistaken for recurring support income?
- Is the proposed housing timeline realistic?

Those answers can give the client and attorney better information before they rely on a particular housing outcome.

## A Practical Example

Assume Sarah earns $6,000 per month from employment and expects to receive $2,500 per month in child support.

She wants to purchase a home two months after the divorce is final.

A basic preapproval that simply adds $2,500 to Sarah’s employment income may overstate what is currently usable.

We first need to review:

1. The decree or qualifying legal agreement
2. When the payments began
3. The documented receipt history
4. Whether payments have been full and timely
5. How long each portion will continue
6. The requirements of the proposed loan program
7. Whether Sarah can qualify using employment income alone

Perhaps Sarah qualifies without the child support.

Perhaps she needs more time to establish the required history.

Perhaps a different loan program has requirements that better fit the circumstances.

The point is not to assume.

The point is to model the actual scenario before Sarah makes an offer.

## What Documents Should You Keep?

If support may be used for mortgage qualification, keep an organized record of:

- The final divorce decree
- Any separation or support agreement
- Court orders or other legally binding documentation
- Bank statements showing deposits
- Canceled checks
- Electronic transfer records
- Records of any late, missing, partial, or catch-up payments
- Documentation showing when payments change or end

Avoid relying on undocumented cash payments.

A clean payment trail can make the mortgage review much more straightforward.

## A Note for Divorce Attorneys and Mediators

Support terms can affect more than a monthly household budget.

They may affect whether either spouse can qualify to keep the marital home or purchase another property.

When housing is an important part of the proposed settlement, useful mortgage questions include:

- Does the receiving spouse need support income to qualify?
- Will the contemplated purchase happen before an acceptable receipt history exists?
- Will the income continue long enough under the anticipated loan program?
- How will the obligation affect the paying spouse’s debt-to-income analysis?
- Can either spouse qualify under the proposed terms?
- Should a Mortgage Feasibility Review be completed before the housing plan is finalized?

My role is not to recommend legal terms.

My role is to help determine whether the proposed mortgage outcome appears financeable.

That information can be valuable before the agreement creates deadlines or expectations that may not work under mortgage guidelines.

## Review the Mortgage Plan Before You Rely on the Income

Child support or alimony may help someone qualify for a mortgage after divorce.

But the lender may need more than a sentence in the divorce decree.

The payment may need to be documented, received consistently, and expected to continue long enough under the applicable loan program.

For the paying spouse, the obligation may reduce future borrowing power.

Before deciding who will keep the house, when someone will buy again, or how quickly a refinance must happen, complete a mortgage feasibility review using the proposed support terms.

The earlier we evaluate it, the more options the client and attorney may have.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can child support count as income for a mortgage?

Potentially. The support generally must be properly documented, received consistently for the period required by the applicable loan program, and expected to continue long enough. Requirements vary by loan program and borrower circumstances.

### Do I have to disclose child support on my mortgage application?

You generally do not have to disclose child support, alimony, or separate-maintenance income if you do not want the lender to consider it for repayment of the loan.

### Is a divorce decree enough to use child support as income?

Not always. The decree may document the obligation, but the lender may also require evidence that payments have actually been received in full, regularly, and on time.

### How long do I need to receive child support before it can count?

For current Fannie Mae conventional guidance, a minimum six-month history is generally required. Other loan programs or circumstances may have different requirements.

### How long must child support or alimony continue?

Under current Fannie Mae conventional guidance, the income generally must be expected to continue for at least three years from the mortgage note date. Other programs may differ.

### Does paying child support affect mortgage qualification?

It can. A continuing court-ordered child-support obligation may be included in the borrower’s recurring monthly obligations and can affect the debt-to-income ratio.

### Can I use an equity-buyout payment as monthly qualifying income?

Do not assume that an equity buyout or lump-sum property settlement will be treated as recurring qualifying income. The lender must review the nature, structure, documentation, and applicable program requirements.
