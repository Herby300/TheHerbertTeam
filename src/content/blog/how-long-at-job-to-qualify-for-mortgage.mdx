One of the most common employment questions I hear from homebuyers is:

**“Do I need to be at the same job for two years before I can qualify for a mortgage?”**

Usually, no.

The idea that every borrower needs two full years with the same employer is one of the most persistent mortgage myths.

Lenders generally care about something broader: whether your employment and income show a stable, documentable, and reasonably predictable pattern. Federal ability-to-repay rules require most lenders to make a reasonable, good-faith determination that you can repay the loan, and your employment status and income are part of that review ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-ability-to-repay-rule-en-1787/)).

That means a recent job change may be perfectly acceptable. In some situations, a borrower may even qualify using an executed offer letter for employment that has not started yet.

The details matter, especially when the new job changes how you are paid.

> **Quick answer:** You may not need two years at the same job to qualify for a mortgage. Under current Fannie Mae conventional guidance, lenders evaluate whether your work history shows a reliable pattern of employment over the most recent two years, and a shorter history may be acceptable when positive factors reasonably offset it. Frequent job changes may also be acceptable when you continue to earn consistent and predictable income. FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, and other loan programs, as well as individual lenders, can have different requirements.

Here is how lenders generally evaluate the most common employment situations.

## The Two-Year Rule Is About the Overall History, Not Necessarily One Employer

Current Fannie Mae guidance says lenders must evaluate whether the borrower’s work history reflects a reliable pattern of employment over the most recent two years ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-3.2-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.2-02/standards-employment-related-income)).

That is different from requiring the borrower to work for one employer for two years.

For example, a borrower might have:

- Worked for Company A for 18 months
- Moved to Company B for a better position
- Received a higher fixed salary
- Remained in the same profession

That job change does not automatically disqualify the borrower.

The lender will review whether the new income is properly documented and likely to continue.

The same guidance also recognizes that borrowers who change jobs frequently may still have a reliable flow of income when their earnings remain consistent and predictable.

## What Does a Lender Actually Review?

When employment income is used to qualify, the lender may review:

- Your current employer
- Start date
- Position or occupation
- Employment history
- Base salary or hourly rate
- Scheduled hours
- Year-to-date earnings
- Prior W-2 income
- Bonus, commission, overtime, or tips
- Whether the income is stable or declining
- Whether the job is expected to continue
- Whether you own part of the business

The type of income often matters as much as the job’s start date.

| Employment situation | Typical mortgage concern |
|---|---|
| New salaried W-2 job | Verify employment, fixed pay, start date, and continuity |
| New hourly job with stable scheduled hours | Verify rate, expected hours, and actual earnings |
| Variable hours or fluctuating base pay | The lender may need an earnings history to calculate a reliable average |
| Commission, overtime, bonus, or tips | Usually requires a history and trend analysis rather than using the highest recent amount |
| New self-employment | Usually requires tax-return history and analysis of the business and income |
| Future job offer | May be eligible under specific program, property, start-date, documentation, and reserve requirements |

This is why two buyers who started new jobs on the same day may receive different mortgage answers.

One may have a fixed salary that can be clearly documented. The other may be relying on commission or variable hours that do not yet have enough history.

## Can You Qualify Right After Starting a New Job?

Potentially, yes.

Assume Michael worked as an accountant for three years and accepts a new accounting position with a higher fixed salary.

He begins the job, receives a paystub, and his employer verifies the position and pay.

Michael does not necessarily need to wait two years, or even several months, simply because the employer changed.

The lender will still review the full file, including:

- His prior employment history
- The new position
- Current pay documentation
- Credit
- Debts
- Assets
- The property
- The selected loan program

The job change itself is not automatically the problem.

The real question is whether the income used for qualification is stable, properly documented, and expected to continue.

## Does the New Job Have to Be in the Same Field?

Not always.

Remaining in the same profession can make the income pattern easier to understand, but changing fields is not automatically disqualifying.

The lender may look more closely at:

- Whether the new compensation is fixed or variable
- Whether the borrower has the education, training, or experience for the position
- Whether there was a significant employment gap
- Whether the new income represents a reasonable and sustainable progression
- Whether the selected mortgage program has additional requirements

A teacher who becomes a salaried corporate trainer may still show a reasonable employment progression.

A borrower who leaves a salaried position to start a brand-new commission-only business presents a different underwriting question.

Do not assume that “new field” means no, or that any new job automatically works. Have the actual offer, compensation structure, and history reviewed.

## Can a Recent Graduate Qualify for a Mortgage?

Potentially.

A recent graduate may not have a traditional two-year employment history because part of that period was spent in school or professional training.

Current Fannie Mae guidance allows a shorter employment history when positive factors reasonably offset it ([B3-3.2-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.2-02/standards-employment-related-income)). The guide does not list those factors, so how a lender weighs your education or training is part of its own underwriting judgment.

Depending on the lender and program, the review may include:

- Degree or training completed
- Relationship between the education and current position
- Executed employment agreement
- Start date
- Fixed versus variable compensation
- Current paystub, when available
- Available assets and reserves
- The full automated or manual underwriting result

This can be especially relevant for newly employed professionals such as teachers, nurses, engineers, attorneys, accountants, or physicians.

The borrower still must meet the applicable credit, debt, asset, property, and loan-program requirements.

## Can You Use an Offer Letter Before Starting the Job?

Sometimes.

Current Fannie Mae guidance provides options for certain borrowers scheduled to begin employment under an executed offer or contract ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-3.3-03](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.3-03/employment-offers-or-contracts)).

The lender generally needs a fully executed offer or contract that identifies:

- The employer
- The borrower
- Position
- Type and rate of pay
- Start date

Fannie Mae currently has two versions of this option:

- **Paystub before loan delivery.** The lender qualifies you using the monthly income stated in the offer, but must obtain a recent paystub supporting that income before the loan is delivered to Fannie Mae.
- **No paystub before loan delivery.** This version is limited to purchases of a one-unit principal residence where you qualify using only fixed base income. The offer must be non-contingent, any conditions of employment must be confirmed as satisfied before closing, and the start date must fall between 30 days before and 90 days after the note date.

The second version also has financial-resource requirements on top of normal reserve requirements. The lender must document either six months of the new home’s principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and assessments (PITIA), or enough eligible financial resources to cover your monthly debts, including the new housing payment, from the note date through the employment start date plus one month ([B3-3.3-03](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.3-03/employment-offers-or-contracts)).

The precise requirements matter. A verbal job promise, contingent offer, commission-only projection, or missing start date may not work. Other loan programs and lenders may not allow offer-letter income at all, or may apply different rules.

Before making an offer on a home, have the lender review the complete employment offer rather than relying on a recruiter’s summary.

## Why Your Pay Structure Matters

A job title does not tell the lender how much income can be used.

Consider two borrowers who each earn approximately $90,000 per year.

### Borrower A

- Fixed salary of $90,000
- Recently changed employers
- Similar occupation
- Fully documented start date and pay

### Borrower B

- $45,000 base salary
- Potential commission of another $45,000
- Started earning commission three months ago

Borrower A may have a more straightforward income analysis because the fixed salary does not depend on a new performance history.

Borrower B may not be able to use the projected commission immediately.

Under current Fannie Mae guidance, a two-year history of bonus, commission, overtime, or tip income is recommended. A shorter history may be considered when it is at least 12 months and positive factors reasonably offset the shorter period ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-3.3-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.3-02/bonus-commission-overtime-and-tip-income)).

The lender also reviews the trend. Stable or increasing variable income may be averaged. Declining income must be shown to have stabilized, and it cannot be used if the decline has not stabilized.

This is why a higher-paying job can temporarily result in less qualifying income when a large portion of the compensation is new and variable.

## Employment Gaps Do Not Automatically Mean “No”

An employment gap can lead to additional questions, but it is not always disqualifying.

Fannie Mae guidance notes that gaps in the most recent 12 months may make employment appear unstable, so the lender must carefully analyze whether your current employment is likely to continue ([B3-3.2-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.2-02/standards-employment-related-income)).

The lender may ask:

- Why did the gap occur?
- How long did it last?
- What did you do before the gap?
- How long have you been back at work?
- Is the current position full-time, part-time, temporary, or permanent?
- Is the current income likely to continue?

Examples might include returning to work after:

- School or professional training
- Caring for a child or family member
- A layoff
- Medical leave
- Relocation
- Military service

The answer depends on the current income, previous history, reason for the gap, documentation, and mortgage program.

Do not delay speaking with a lender because you assume the gap automatically prevents approval.

## Switching From W-2 Employment to Self-Employment Is Different

This is one of the most important distinctions.

Leaving one salaried W-2 job for another salaried W-2 job may be relatively straightforward.

Leaving a W-2 job to become self-employed usually requires a different analysis.

Fannie Mae generally requires a two-year history of prior self-employment earnings. A borrower with less than two years may be considered when the most recent signed personal and business tax returns reflect a full 12 months of income from the current business and the file supports prior income at the same or higher level in a field offering the same products or services, or in an occupation with similar responsibilities ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-3.5-01](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.5-01/underwriting-factors-and-documentation-self-employed-borrower)).

That means a borrower who starts a business last month usually cannot qualify by simply annualizing the first month’s deposits.

The lender may need tax returns, a profit-and-loss statement, business documentation, and a cash-flow analysis.

If you expect to leave W-2 employment and buy a home soon, complete the mortgage review before making the employment change.

## Do Not Change Jobs During the Mortgage Process Without Calling

Even when the new job is better, changing employment between preapproval and closing can create additional work or delay.

The lender may need to:

- Recalculate qualifying income
- Obtain a new offer letter or paystub
- Verify the new employer
- Re-run automated underwriting
- Review new commission, bonus, overtime, or variable-hour income
- Confirm the start date
- Update the closing timeline

Current Fannie Mae guidance requires a late-stage verbal verification of employment for borrowers using employment-related income, generally within 10 business days before the note date. The purpose is to confirm, as late in the process as possible, that the borrower is still employed ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-3.1-04](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.1-04/verbal-verification-employment)).

The safest approach is simple:

**Call your loan officer before accepting, starting, leaving, or materially changing a job during the mortgage process.**

This does not mean you should turn down a good career opportunity. It means the mortgage strategy should be updated before the employment change becomes a closing surprise.

## Documents to Gather After a Job Change

Depending on the situation, be ready to provide:

- Recent paystubs
- W-2 forms
- Employment offer or contract
- Employer contact information
- Written verification of employment
- Explanation of an employment gap
- Documentation of salary, hourly rate, scheduled hours, commission, bonus, or overtime
- Tax returns when the income type requires them
- Bank or asset statements if reserves are part of an offer-letter strategy

Do not edit or recreate employer documents. Provide the original documentation and allow the lender to verify it independently.

## A Practical Preapproval Strategy

If you recently changed jobs, or expect to, ask the lender to evaluate these questions before you shop:

1. Which parts of my compensation can be used today?
2. Does my work history support the new income?
3. Is any portion variable and subject to an averaging requirement?
4. Do I need a paystub before closing?
5. Can an offer letter be used under my loan program?
6. Are reserves required because my job starts after closing?
7. Will a job change affect my closing date?
8. What final employment verification will be completed?

A strong preapproval is not just a calculation based on the annual salary typed into an application.

It should reflect the documentation and income treatment the underwriter is actually expected to use.

## The Bottom Line

You may not need two years at the same job to qualify for a mortgage.

What matters is whether the lender can document a reliable employment and income pattern under the selected loan program.

A new fixed-salary job may work quickly. A future employment offer may work under specific conditions. New commission, variable hours, or self-employment may require more history.

The timing of the mortgage review is especially important if you are:

- Starting a new job
- Changing employers
- Returning to work after a gap
- Graduating and beginning a career
- Moving from salary to commission
- Becoming self-employed
- Relocating for work

Before assuming you must wait, or that your new salary will automatically be accepted, have the full employment situation reviewed. A confidential **Mortgage Feasibility Review** can look at your actual offer, pay structure, and work history before you shop for a home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need two years at the same job to get a mortgage?

Usually, no. Lenders generally evaluate the overall employment and income history, not simply the time with one employer. The income must still be stable, documentable, and eligible under the selected program.

### Can I get a mortgage if I just started a new job?

Potentially. A new salaried or otherwise stable W-2 position may be acceptable when the lender can verify the job, pay, start date, and employment history. Variable income may require more history.

### Can I qualify with an offer letter?

Potentially. Some loan programs permit qualifying with a fully executed employment offer when specific requirements for the transaction, property, compensation, start date, verification, and financial reserves are satisfied.

### Can a recent college graduate qualify for a mortgage?

Potentially. A shorter work history may be acceptable when positive factors support it. The lender will review the education or training, current employment, pay structure, and complete loan file.

### Does a gap in employment prevent mortgage approval?

Not automatically. The lender will analyze the reason and length of the gap, prior employment, time back at work, current income, and likelihood that the employment will continue.

### Can I use commission or bonus income from a new job?

Maybe not immediately. Current Fannie Mae guidance recommends a two-year history for bonus, commission, overtime, and tip income. A history of at least 12 months may be considered with positive offsetting factors. The lender also evaluates the income trend.

### How long must I be self-employed to qualify?

Fannie Mae generally requires two years of prior self-employment earnings. A shorter history may be eligible when the current business has generated a full 12 months of income shown on your most recent tax returns and you have prior income at the same or higher level in a related field or occupation. Other programs may differ.

### Should I change jobs before closing?

Do not make the change without speaking with your loan officer. A job change can require new documentation, income calculations, employment verification, underwriting, or a revised closing timeline.
