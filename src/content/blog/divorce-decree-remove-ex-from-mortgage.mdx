A divorce decree can award the marital home to one spouse.

It can require that spouse to make the mortgage payments.

It can even set a deadline for refinancing the loan.

But the divorce decree does **not automatically remove the other spouse from the existing mortgage.**

That is one of the most important distinctions in divorce mortgage planning.

The divorce decree governs obligations between the former spouses. The mortgage note is a separate contract with the lender or loan servicer.

According to the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-contact-me-about-a-debt-after-a-divorce-en-1413/), a creditor may generally continue holding anyone named as a borrower responsible unless the creditor contractually releases that person or the debt is refinanced without them.

> **Quick answer:** Awarding the house to one spouse, transferring title, and removing a borrower from the mortgage are three separate events. Completing one does not automatically complete the others.

## Title, Mortgage Liability, and the Divorce Decree Are Different

It helps to separate the documents involved.

| Document or action | What it generally controls | What it does not automatically do |
|---|---|---|
| Deed or title transfer | Who owns an interest in the property | Remove a borrower from the mortgage debt |
| Mortgage note | Who promised to repay the loan | Determine how the divorce court divides property |
| Deed of trust or mortgage | Gives the lender a security interest in the property | Release a borrower from the note |
| Divorce decree or settlement | Establishes obligations between the former spouses | Rewrite the creditor’s original loan contract |
| Release of liability | Formally releases an existing borrower when approved | Transfer ownership by itself |
| Refinance | Pays off the old mortgage with a new loan | Guarantee that the remaining spouse will qualify |

This is why signing a deed is not the same as being removed from the loan.

A spouse can give up ownership of the home and still remain responsible for the mortgage.

## Why Signing a Quitclaim Deed Is Not Enough

A deed may transfer one spouse’s ownership interest to the spouse keeping the home.

The exact instrument and timing are legal and title matters that should be handled by the divorce attorney and title professional.

From the mortgage side, however, the key point is straightforward:

**Changing title does not automatically change the borrowers on the mortgage note.**

If both spouses signed the note, both may remain contractually responsible after the title transfer.

That can create a painful mismatch:

- One former spouse no longer owns the home
- That spouse cannot control whether payments are made
- The mortgage may still appear on that spouse’s credit
- A late payment may affect both borrowers
- The debt may complicate qualification for another home

A deed should not be presented as a substitute for refinancing, an approved assumption with release of liability, or paying off the loan.

## What Does the Divorce Decree Accomplish?

The decree can establish which spouse is responsible to the other spouse for making payments, refinancing, selling the property, or completing another required action.

But the lender was not rewritten out of its contract simply because the spouses’ responsibilities changed.

The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-contact-me-about-a-debt-after-a-divorce-en-1413/) explains that sending a creditor a divorce decree does not end a borrower’s responsibility for a joint debt.

If the spouse ordered to pay stops making payments, the other borrower may have remedies under the divorce decree. Those remedies are legal questions for the divorce attorney.

They do not necessarily prevent the mortgage servicer from reporting a delinquency or pursuing a borrower who remains obligated on the note.

## Four Ways the Mortgage May Ultimately Be Resolved

The correct approach depends on the loan, equity, income, credit, interest rate, decree, and long-term housing plan.

### 1. Refinance Into the Keeping Spouse’s Name

A refinance pays off the existing mortgage and replaces it with a new loan.

If only the spouse keeping the home signs the new note, the paid-off mortgage no longer leaves the departing spouse obligated on that debt.

The keeping spouse must qualify for the new loan.

The lender may review:

- Employment and qualifying income
- Credit history and scores
- Monthly debts
- Child-support or spousal-support obligations
- Eligible support income
- Property taxes
- Homeowners insurance
- HOA dues
- Appraised value
- Existing mortgage payoff
- Equity being paid to the departing spouse
- Available assets and reserves
- The requirements of the selected loan program

A decree cannot make an otherwise unaffordable refinance qualify.

That is why the mortgage review should occur before the settlement depends on a refinance deadline.

### 2. Complete an Approved Assumption and Release of Liability

Some mortgage loans may permit an assumption or transfer process.

An assumption generally allows another person or remaining owner to take responsibility for the existing loan under the applicable servicing and program requirements.

But there is an essential second question:

**Will the departing borrower receive a formal release of liability?**

An ownership transfer or assumption without a release may leave the original borrower responsible.

For loans owned by Fannie Mae, current [servicing guidance](https://servicing-guide.fanniemae.com/svc/f-1-17/processing-transfer-ownership) treats an assumption and an assumption with a release of liability as separate outcomes. Before approving a release, the servicer must find that the remaining homeowner’s credit and financial capacity are acceptable. If the loan has mortgage insurance and the insurer doesn’t agree to the release, the release is denied, although the ownership transfer itself may still go through ([Fannie Mae Servicing Guide F-1-28](https://servicing-guide.fanniemae.com/svc/f-1-28/reviewing-transfer-ownership-credit-and-financial-capacity)). Other investors and loan programs have their own rules.

Mortgage insurance, loan type, investor rules, payment history, occupancy, and servicer procedures may also matter.

Do not assume a loan is assumable—or that assumption automatically releases the departing spouse. Obtain the servicer’s requirements and written confirmation.

### 3. Sell the Home and Pay Off the Mortgage

If neither spouse can qualify to retain the property—or keeping it does not make financial sense—a sale may provide the cleanest mortgage resolution.

At closing, the sale proceeds generally pay off the existing mortgage and other liens before the remaining equity is distributed under the settlement and closing instructions.

The divorce attorney and title professional should coordinate ownership, authority to sell, required signatures, proceeds, and any legal restrictions.

The mortgage professional can help each spouse assess future purchase qualification and timing.

### 4. Leave the Existing Mortgage in Place Temporarily

Some settlements allow one spouse to remain in the home while the mortgage stays jointly obligated for a defined period.

There may be personal reasons for considering this, including children, school schedules, market conditions, or interest-rate differences.

But this arrangement carries real risk for the departing spouse.

The mortgage can remain on that person’s credit. Late payments may affect both borrowers. The obligation may complicate another home purchase. The departing spouse also depends on someone else to protect a debt still reported in both names.

If the parties are considering a temporary arrangement, the attorneys should address matters such as:

- Who makes the mortgage payment
- How payment confirmation will be shared
- Who pays taxes, insurance, HOA dues, and repairs
- Whether escrow shortages or special assessments are covered
- What happens after a late or missed payment
- The refinance or sale deadline
- What happens if the refinance is denied
- Whether extensions are permitted
- The backup sale process
- Access to mortgage and insurance information

Those are legal drafting considerations. A mortgage professional should not draft the settlement language.

## Does a Divorce Transfer Trigger the Due-on-Sale Clause?

A title transfer and a release from mortgage liability are still different questions.

[Federal law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1701j-3) limits enforcement of a due-on-sale clause for certain transfers involving residential property with fewer than five units. One protected category includes a qualifying transfer resulting from a divorce decree, legal-separation agreement, or incidental property settlement through which the borrower’s spouse becomes an owner.

That protection can affect whether the lender may call the loan immediately due because of the transfer.

It does **not** automatically release an existing borrower from the mortgage debt.

The attorney and servicer should confirm how the law and loan documents apply to the specific transfer.

## What If the Departing Spouse Wants to Buy Another Home?

Remaining on the old mortgage does not always mean the departing spouse can never qualify for another home.

But the existing obligation must be reviewed under the selected loan program.

Current [Fannie Mae conventional guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-06/qualifying-impact-other-real-estate-owned) contains a specific property-settlement-buyout provision. When one co-owner buys out another owner’s interest but the lender does not release the departing borrower, the borrower has a contingent liability. Under that guidance, the payment may not need to be included in recurring monthly debts when the lender documents the transfer of title.

That is potentially helpful, but it is not universal.

It does not remove the borrower from the loan. It does not prevent late payments from affecting credit. Other conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, portfolio, and non-QM programs may apply different requirements.

The new lender must review the actual decree, title transfer, credit report, payment history, and applicable guidelines before deciding how the old mortgage will be treated.

## A Practical Example

Assume Chris and Taylor are both borrowers on a $400,000 mortgage.

Their divorce decree awards the home to Taylor and requires Taylor to make the payments and refinance within 12 months.

Chris signs the required deed and is removed from title.

Has Chris been removed from the mortgage?

No.

Until Taylor completes an approved refinance, assumption with release of liability, or another payoff, Chris may remain a borrower on the existing loan.

If Taylor misses a payment, Chris’s credit may still be affected.

If Chris wants to purchase another home, the new lender must determine how the existing mortgage should be treated under the selected loan program and documentation.

The better sequence would have been to complete a Mortgage Feasibility Review before finalizing a settlement that depends on Taylor qualifying alone.

That review could have tested:

1. Whether Taylor’s income supports the existing home
2. Whether support payments help or hurt qualification
3. The likely new loan amount and payment
4. Whether an equity buyout must be financed
5. Whether an assumption and release may be available
6. Whether refinancing within the proposed deadline is realistic
7. What backup plan is needed if the first option fails

The review does not decide who receives the house.

It helps determine whether the proposed mortgage outcome is realistically financeable.

## Questions to Ask the Mortgage Servicer

If assumption or release of liability is being considered, ask the current servicer:

1. Who owns or guarantees the mortgage?
2. Is this transfer treated as an exempt divorce-related transfer?
3. Is the loan eligible for an assumption?
4. What is required for a release of liability?
5. Must the remaining homeowner qualify based on current income, credit, debts, and assets?
6. Does mortgage insurance approval apply?
7. Will the loan terms or interest rate change?
8. What documents are required?
9. How long does the review usually take?
10. Will the servicer provide written confirmation when the departing borrower is released?

Keep copies of all submissions, correspondence, approvals, and recorded documents.

## A Note for Divorce Attorneys and Mediators

When one spouse wants to retain the marital home, the settlement should not rely solely on:

**“The client will refinance later.”**

Before finalizing the housing provision, useful mortgage-planning questions include:

- Can the keeping spouse qualify using individually available income?
- Must support be paid or received before it can be documented?
- How much equity must be paid to the departing spouse?
- Does the refinance amount fit applicable loan-to-value limits?
- What happens to the monthly payment at current rates?
- Could an assumption and release preserve the existing loan?
- Has the servicer confirmed its process?
- Is the proposed deadline realistic?
- What happens if the refinance or release is denied?
- Can the departing spouse qualify for another home during the transition?
- Does the backup plan require sale of the property?

The attorney determines the client’s legal rights and drafts the decree.

The mortgage professional determines whether the financing strategy appears workable under current lending requirements.

Those two analyses should happen together, but they are not the same service.

## Mortgage Planning Checklist Before Signing the Settlement

Before relying on a home-retention plan, gather:

- Current mortgage statement
- Original note or available loan information
- Names of all borrowers
- Current deed or title information
- Estimated property value
- Mortgage payoff estimate
- Proposed equity division
- Income documentation for the keeping spouse
- Current debt and credit information
- Proposed support obligations
- Homeowners-insurance cost
- Property-tax amount
- HOA dues
- Servicer assumption and release requirements
- Proposed refinance or sale deadline
- Backup plan if financing is unavailable

A Mortgage Feasibility Review can compare refinancing, assumption, sale, and temporary retention before the settlement depends on one path.

## The Bottom Line

A divorce decree can award the house and assign payment responsibility.

It does not automatically remove an ex-spouse from the mortgage.

A deed transfer changes ownership. A divorce decree establishes responsibilities between the spouses. A refinance, approved assumption and release of liability, or payoff addresses the existing loan obligation.

Those steps should not be treated as interchangeable.

If one spouse wants to keep the home, test the mortgage strategy before the settlement is final.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a divorce decree remove my ex-spouse from the mortgage?

No. A divorce decree may assign responsibility for the home and mortgage payments between former spouses, but it does not automatically alter the lender’s loan contract or release a borrower.

### Does removing someone from the deed remove them from the mortgage?

No. Title ownership and mortgage liability are separate. Someone can transfer an ownership interest and remain contractually responsible for the mortgage note.

### Can one spouse refinance without the other?

Potentially. The spouse keeping the home must qualify for the new loan and satisfy the applicable ownership, title, equity, credit, income, property, and mortgage-program requirements.

### Can my spouse assume the mortgage?

Possibly. Assumption eligibility depends on the existing loan, investor or guarantor, servicer, mortgage insurance, and borrower qualification requirements. Confirm whether the process includes a formal release of liability for the departing borrower.

### Is an assumption the same as a release of liability?

Not necessarily. An assumption and a release of liability are separate issues. Ask the servicer whether its approval formally releases the departing borrower, and obtain written confirmation.

### Can I buy another home while my name is on the old mortgage?

Possibly. Your new lender must determine how the existing obligation is treated under the selected loan program. A qualifying property-settlement buyout may receive specific treatment under Fannie Mae guidance, but that does not release you from the old loan or protect your credit from missed payments.

### Does a divorce-related title transfer automatically make the loan due?

Certain qualifying divorce-related transfers receive federal due-on-sale protection. That protection is separate from a release of liability. Ask your attorney and servicer to evaluate your specific transfer.

### When should I request a Mortgage Feasibility Review?

Before signing a settlement that depends on refinancing, an equity buyout, an assumption, or purchasing another home. Earlier review gives the attorney and client time to consider a backup plan.
